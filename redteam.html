Red teaming and securing generative AI, mapped to this site

The course treats generative AI and autonomous agents as the target software stack, not as a security tool. It covers prompt injection, context manipulation, agent hijacking and defensive architecture. Each class has a lecture, a current events segment and a hands-on exercise.     The four labs, and where to practice

    Labs are about 25% of the grade in total. Run every attack only against models and apps you are allowed to test.

    Lab 1. Measuring jailbreaks

    Run manual and automated jailbreaks, such as GCG, PAIR and garak, against open-weight models you are allowed to test, and report attack success rates over many trials, not single anecdotes. A measurement-based alternative exists for students who prefer not to do persuasion-style attacks.

    Practice here. Adversarial prompting lab Jailbreak lab desk

    Lab 2. Escalating agent attacks

    An AgentDojo-style ladder where each level adds a defense. Hijack tool-using agents through direct and indirect injection and record how each defense was bypassed.

    Practice here. Lesson 9 Lesson 2 desk PromptTrace Gauntlet in the plan

    Lab 3. Build and defend an agent

    Teams build a tool-using agent, threat model it, add model-level and architectural defenses, then swap agents and attack each other adaptively.

    Practice here. Defensive prompting lab STRIDE lab Defense design desk

    Lab 4. Poisoning or membership inference

    Either backdoor a small model by fine-tuning and vary the number of poisoned examples, or run a membership inference study and see how distribution shift can make an attack look like it works.

    Practice here. Lesson 7 desk, plan a study Lesson 4 desk

    Thing of the week, a 10 minute template

    Each student presents one recent incident or development. This outline fits the brief.

    1. Pick one recent incident or development from the last month.
    2. Say in one sentence what happened.
    3. Name the course concept it shows, such as indirect injection, the lethal trifecta or poisoning.
    4. Map it to one framework entry, such as an OWASP risk or an ATLAS technique.
    5. Say what defense would have stopped it, and whether that defense is model-level or architectural.
    6. End with one open question for the class.

    Exams

    Both exams are paper and pencil, open note but not open device. The midterm covers weeks 1 to 7. The final is an incident analysis. You read an incident write-up you have not seen, explain what happened with the course frameworks, and recommend fixes.

    To practice, take any incident from the OWASP page or the attack lessons. Map it with STRIDE, ATLAS and OWASP, find the broken trust boundary, then pick a model-level and an architectural fix. The practice desks give instant feedback with a reason for every answer.

    AI use rules worth copying

    The course asks you to keep studying AI separate from replacing your learning with AI. Its rules for assignments are good practice for any security team.

    • Name the model and the date for any AI help, and say what it did and on which part.
    • Keep your prompts and the model's replies, and share them on request.
    • You are responsible for everything an agentic tool does on your behalf, including staying in scope.
    • Do not paste another team's code, prompts or secrets into a third party AI service without permission.
    • Models you attack, defend or evaluate are part of your methods, not your AI use disclosure.

    Summarized from the course syllabus. AI tools are not allowed in the exams. The syllabus is a draft and may change, so check the official course page for the current version.
