MITRE ATLAS, the attack map for AI systems

A public knowledge base of how real attackers target AI and machine learning systems, organized as tactics, techniques, mitigations and case studies.

The idea in one minute

ATLAS stands for Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems. MITRE, a US non-profit, publishes it for free at atlas.mitre.org.

It answers one question. What do attackers actually do to AI systems? Every entry comes from real incidents, red team work or published research.

Think of it like this

Think of a field guide to burglars. It lists the steps they take (case the house, get in, find the safe), and under each step the tricks they use. ATLAS is that guide, for people who break into AI systems.

Watch it, step by step

Press Next or Play. Each step lights up one part. Arrow keys move steps after you click the diagram.

Words you need

Tactic
The attacker's goal at one stage, the why. For example Initial Access. ATLAS tactic IDs look like AML.TA0004.
Technique
How the attacker reaches that goal, the how. For example LLM Prompt Injection, AML.T0051.
Sub-technique
A more specific form of a technique. For example AML.T0051.001, Indirect prompt injection.
Mitigation
A defense that reduces a technique. ATLAS mitigation IDs look like AML.M0000.
Case study
A real or researched attack, written as a sequence of ATLAS techniques.
Matrix
The grid view. Tactics are columns, techniques sit under them.

How it works, step by step

Step 1. Tactics are the columns

Release 2026.10 has 16 tactics, in roughly the order an attack happens.

  1. Reconnaissance
  2. Resource Development
  3. Initial Access
  4. AI Model Access, only in ATLAS
  5. Execution
  6. Persistence
  7. Privilege Escalation
  8. Defense Evasion
  9. Credential Access
  10. Discovery
  11. Lateral Movement
  12. Collection
  13. AI Attack Staging, only in ATLAS
  14. Command and Control
  15. Exfiltration
  16. Impact

The two ATLAS-only tactics are the heart of it. AI Model Access is how the attacker gets to the model, for example through its public API. AI Attack Staging is where the attacker prepares the AI-specific attack, for example by crafting adversarial data or training a copy of the model.

Step 2. Techniques sit under tactics

Release 2026.10 lists about 130 techniques and about 90 sub-techniques. One technique can serve more than one tactic.

Step 3. Case studies show whole attacks

Each case study is a story written as a chain of techniques. Reading them teaches you how techniques combine.

Step 4. Mitigations close the loop

Each technique page links to mitigations that reduce it. ATLAS mitigations are often process controls, such as limiting model access or validating models before use.

Fact check

Some IDs change between releases. In release 2026.10, the old system prompt technique AML.T0056 is now AML.T0069.002, and training data extraction is under AML.T0137. Always check the ID on the live site.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Pick the system you care about, such as a RAG chatbot or an ML malware classifier.
  2. Walk the tactics left to right. For each, ask which techniques could apply to your system.
  3. Mark the techniques you can detect, the ones you can prevent, and the gaps.
  4. Read two or three case studies close to your system.
  5. Tag your detection rules and test cases with technique IDs, so reports speak one language.
  6. Revisit after each ATLAS release, because IDs and techniques change.

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Built from real incidents and research, not guesses.
  • Same structure as ATT&CK, so security teams already know how to read it.
  • Free, with machine-readable data on GitHub.

Limits

  • It describes attacker behavior. It does not tell you which risks matter most for your app. Use OWASP and a threat model for that.
  • IDs and names change between releases. Pin the release you used in reports.
  • Coverage follows published cases, so brand-new attack classes appear later.
