Misinformation, hallucination and provenance

Why models state false things with confidence, how attackers use that at scale, and what watermarks and signed content credentials can and cannot prove.

The idea in one minute

An LLM writes likely text, not checked text. When it does not know, it can still write a fluent, specific answer. This is hallucination, and it is part of how the model works.

Misinformation becomes a security risk in two ways.

  • By accident. People and programs act on false output, such as a wrong CVE version or a package name that does not exist.
  • On purpose. Attackers use models to write persuasive false content at scale, or plant false data for models to repeat.
Think of it like this

A student who always answers, even when they do not know, sounds the same when right and when wrong. You can tell only by checking the book.

Words you need

Hallucination
Fluent output that is false or not supported by any source.
Overreliance
Trusting model output without checking it.
Slopsquatting
Registering a package name that AI assistants invent, so developers install the attacker's package.
Watermark
A hidden statistical signal placed in generated content so a detector can find it later.
Content credentials
A signed record attached to a file that says who made it and how. C2PA defines the format.
Provenance
The history of where content came from and how it changed.

How it works, step by step

Why the model is confident and wrong

Module 5 of the defensive course shows this with a real model. Asked about CVE-2024-3400, a small model named the wrong product, Apache Struts, with 46 percent probability on the first word. When it named the product again later in the same answer, its probability rose to 93 percent, because its own first guess was now part of the text. Confidence rose. Truth did not.

Misinformation as a weapon

The Stanford HAI brief summarizes a study by Goldstein and others. GPT-3 wrote news-style articles that were nearly as persuasive as real foreign propaganda. With light human editing, they matched it. Models make this content cheap and fast.

Package hallucination

Spracklen and others (2024) tested code models and found that about one in five recommended packages did not exist. Many invented names came back again and again. Module 6 cites that 43 percent of invented names came back in all 10 of 10 tries. An attacker can register those names with malware.

Watermarks and signatures

  • A statistical watermark, such as Google's SynthID, hides a pattern in the content itself. It survives some edits, such as compression. Heavy changes, such as paraphrasing or translating text, can remove it.
  • C2PA content credentials attach a signed manifest to a file. The signature proves the record is intact. But the record is metadata, and a screenshot or re-upload often strips it.

The attack, as a workflow

  1. Ask models the questions your targets will ask. Record names, links and facts they invent.
  2. Register invented package names or domains, or publish pages that confirm the false facts.
  3. For influence operations, generate many variants of a message and post them through many accounts.
  4. Wait for people or pipelines to trust and act on the output.

Real cases

Air Canada chatbot, 2024

A tribunal in Canada held Air Canada responsible after its website chatbot gave a customer wrong refund information. The company could not blame the bot.

Lawyers and fake citations, 2023

In Mata v. Avianca in New York, lawyers filed a brief with court cases that ChatGPT invented. The court sanctioned them.

How to defend

  • Ground answers in trusted sources with RAG, and show the sources.
  • Check key facts against the source that owns them, such as NVD for CVEs and the vendor for fixes.
  • Check that a package exists, is popular and is the right one before you install it. Use lock files and an approved package list.
  • Ask a person to review high-stakes output.
  • Show users that answers can be wrong, and make checking easy.
  • For provenance, combine methods. Use watermarks, signed credentials and platform labels together. None works alone.
