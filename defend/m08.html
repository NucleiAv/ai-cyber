AI on the cyber battlefield

How AI really changes an attack, which defensive signals survive a perfect fake, how to read a threat report like an analyst, and where a defender with a fixed budget should spend first.

The idea in one minute

Every attacker headline now says "AI". The useful question is narrower. Which resource of the attack did AI change, and by how much?

This module uses four levers. Skill, cost, time and scale. Rate each one before AI and with AI. Spend where the gap is biggest, and only after independent evidence shows the capability is real.

Think of it like this

A new power tool does not make every burglar dangerous. It makes one job faster, such as cutting locks. If you know which job got easier, you know which door to reinforce.

Learn it from zero

Step 1. The four levers

  • Skill. The expertise the attacker needs. AI lowers it when a model does what a person had to know, such as writing fluent English.
  • Cost. Money and infrastructure. A subscription can replace a paid specialist.
  • Time. From start to result. A model drafts and sorts faster.
  • Scale. Targets per operator. Hand-made output becomes automatic.

Step 2. Social engineering at machine scale

A model writes a flawless, tailored message from public information. So spelling and grammar are no longer evidence. In January 2024, a finance worker at Arup in Hong Kong joined a video call where the CFO and colleagues were all deepfakes, and made 15 transfers of about 25.6 million US dollars.

Signals that survive a perfect fake do not depend on reading the fake. Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) still fails for a forged sender. An out-of-pattern request stays odd. A callback on a known number and a second approver need a step the attacker cannot complete. Deepfake detectors help, but they lose accuracy on new fakes.

Step 3. Recon and vulnerability discovery

Attackers and defenders both feed scan output to models. Models summarize well but can overflow their context and invent exploits. Some research systems, such as Google's Big Sleep, have found real new vulnerabilities.

Step 4. Read reports, separate claims

Split each report into capability claims (what the AI did) and attribution claims (who was behind it). They rest on different evidence. Name the author's incentive in both directions. A caveat the author makes against their own headline is strong evidence.

Step 5. Where autonomy is allowed

Judge each automated step with four criteria. Authorization, scope, reversibility and accountability. Steps that only produce information can run alone with logging. Steps that cannot be undone, such as running an exploit or copying data out, need a human gate.

Words you need

Lever
One resource an attack uses. Skill, cost, time or scale.
Baseline
How the attack worked before AI.
Dual use
The same capability serves defense and offense.
Out-of-band
Confirming a request through a separate channel set up in advance.
Capability claim
What the AI could do and how much of the work it did.
Attribution claim
Who was behind an operation.
Autonomy ceiling
The current limit on what offensive AI can do end to end without a human.
