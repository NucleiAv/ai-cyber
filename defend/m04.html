Intrusion detection, log analysis and AI triage

How a SOC turns raw logs into alerts, where machine learning and LLMs fit in triage, how they fail, and how to judge a detector with the numbers your own team can afford.

The idea in one minute

A security operations center (SOC) gets thousands of alerts a day and has a few analysts. Triage is deciding which alerts a person looks at, and how soon.

Tools can help at every step. Parsers read logs, signature engines and ML detectors raise alerts, and LLMs can summarize and label them. Each tool fails in its own way. This module teaches you to check machine output against ground truth, and to choose a detector by what it costs your team.

Think of it like this

Triage in a hospital decides who sees a doctor first. If the nurse's computer says "minor" for a heart attack, nobody rushes. In a SOC, a "Low" label on a real attack does the same.

Learn it from zero

Step 1. Logs and parsing

Devices write logs in formats such as syslog and CEF (Common Event Format). A parser pulls out fields, such as source IP, destination port and action. Ground truth for a field is what the log line itself contains. If a tool reports a value that is not in the line, it invented it.

Step 2. Detection at wire speed

Network detection must keep up with traffic. Signature engines such as Snort and Suricata match rules in real time. Flow records summarize connections into numbers, such as bytes, packets and duration. ML detectors read those numbers. Supervised models learn known attacks. Anomaly detection flags what looks unusual.

Step 3. The four-step triage pipeline

  1. Parse. Extract fields from the raw alert.
  2. Classify. Give a triage severity, such as Critical, High, Medium, Low or Informational.
  3. Enrich. Add context to Critical and High alerts.
  4. Hand off. Pass alerts to analysts, most severe first.

The label from step 2 decides whether a person ever sees the alert.

Step 4. Five ways LLM triage fails

  • Hallucinated IOC. Names an IP, domain or hash that is not in the log.
  • Severity inflation. Calls a routine event high because its words sound like an attack.
  • Severity deflation. Calls a real threat low, so no analyst looks. This is the most dangerous.
  • Context fabrication. Gives a confident explanation the log does not support.
  • Knowledge cutoff. Cannot recognize tools or CVEs newer than its training data.

Step 5. Measure with your own budget

A vendor's precision and recall mean different things for a team of 3 and a team of 20. Work out how many investigations a day the detector creates, and how many attacks it misses. For detectors with a tunable threshold, compare them at the false positive rate your team can afford, not by AUC alone.

Words you need

SOC
Security operations center. The team that watches and responds to alerts.
CEF
Common Event Format. A log format with a pipe-separated header and key=value extension.
Syslog PRI
The number in angle brackets at the start of a syslog line. It packs facility and severity.
Ground truth
The real value, checked against the evidence itself.
Beacon
A compromised host that calls its attacker's server on a regular schedule.
Deterministic
Same input, same output, every time.
Stochastic
Output is sampled, so repeated runs can differ.
TPR
True positive rate. Attacks caught divided by real attacks. The same as recall.
AUC
Area under the ROC curve. One score across every threshold, from 0.5 (guessing) to 1.0.
Concept drift
Traffic changes over time, so a trained detector gets worse.
