How AI transforms the threat landscape by lowering barriers to entry, compressing attack timelines, and introducing novel attack surfaces.

1

Three Structural Shifts in the Threat Landscape

AI does not create new categories of attack. Phishing, malware, social engineering, and vulnerability exploitation all predate AI. The structural impact is more fundamental: AI transforms who can conduct attacks, how quickly attack chains execute, and what constitutes a viable target.

Lowered Barriers to Entry

AI lowers the barrier to entry for cybercrime. Tasks that once required years of expertise can now be performed by novices with access to the right tools. NCC Group tracked 124 distinct ransomware groups in 2025, up 50% year over year, driven largely by AI-powered platforms that let anyone with a subscription launch sophisticated campaigns.

Compressed Attack Timelines

The CrowdStrike 2026 Global Threat Report found that the average time from initial access to lateral movement dropped to 29 minutes, with the fastest observed breakout at just 27 seconds. In one intrusion, data exfiltration began within four minutes. Traditional incident response playbooks assume hours, not seconds.

Novel Attack Surfaces

AI systems themselves become targets. CrowdStrike documented adversaries injecting malicious prompts into GenAI tools at over 90 organizations, exploiting AI development platforms to deploy ransomware, and publishing fake AI servers that impersonated trusted services to intercept sensitive data.

Case Study: The $11 Voice Clone

UC Berkeley's Center for Long-Term Cybersecurity (CLTC) documented that criminals can now clone a person's voice using about an hour of YouTube footage and an $11/month subscription. Researchers found attackers using AI-generated identities to apply for remote jobs at tech firms, passing background checks with fabricated histories and deepfake video interviews. Once hired, these fake "employees" gained privileged access to internal systems. This is not a theoretical risk. It is a documented attack pattern that did not exist before generative AI made it economically viable.

Source: University of California, Berkeley, Center for Long-Term Cybersecurity (CLTC).

Dual-Use Implications: Offense and Defense

Every AI capability in cybersecurity is . The same underlying technology serves attackers and defenders. But the advantages are not symmetric.

Defensive Applications

Data volume
Legitimate organizations generate massive datasets of normal behavior, giving ML models rich training material for anomaly detection.
Infrastructure scale
Defenders can deploy AI at the network edge, email gateway, and endpoint simultaneously.
Organizational context
Defenders know their own environment. Attackers must discover it.
Adoption is growing
64% of organizations now assess the security of AI tools before deployment, up from 37% in 2025 (WEF 2026).

Offensive Applications

Faster feedback loops
Attackers know immediately if a phishing email was clicked or a payload executed. Defenders often learn about breaches weeks later.
Lower cost to entry
AI-powered RaaS platforms and $11 voice cloning subscriptions mean sophisticated attacks no longer require sophisticated attackers.
Only need to succeed once
Defenders must protect every entry point. Attackers only need one to work.
AI systems as targets
Adversaries are now attacking the AI tools defenders rely on, creating a new category of risk.

Asymmetric Impact Across Organizations

The net impact of AI on organizational security posture is context-dependent and actively debated in the research community. Well-resourced organizations with mature security programs can operationalize AI's defensive capabilities effectively. Under-resourced organizations face disproportionate risk because attacker-side AI tools are inexpensive, require minimal expertise, and need no dedicated infrastructure. The WEF 2026 report identifies this as "widening cyber inequity": the gap between organizations that can defend themselves and those that cannot is growing, and AI is accelerating this divergence.

Evaluating AI Security Claims

Virtually every cybersecurity vendor now describes their products as "AI-powered." The majority of these claims lack specificity, rely on self-reported metrics, and are designed for marketing rather than technical evaluation. A structured framework for evaluating AI claims is essential for any security professional. The following five questions apply to any AI claim you encounter, in this course and throughout your career.

What TYPE of AI? Rule-based? Classical ML? Deep learning? LLM? If the vendor cannot specify, they may not understand their own product.
What METRIC, on what DATA, vs. what BASELINE? "99.5% accuracy" means nothing without context. On what dataset? With what class balance? Compared to random guessing, the previous product, or a human analyst?
Who EVALUATED it? Vendor self-reported? Independent lab? Peer-reviewed? A vendor testing their own product is like a student grading their own exam.
What are the FAILURE MODES? Every system fails. If a vendor cannot describe how theirs fails, they do not understand it.
What CHANGES between test and deployment? A model tested on 2023 attack data and deployed against 2026 attackers has a validity gap. Base rates change, traffic patterns shift, adversaries adapt.

Try It: Apply the Framework

CrowdStrike 2026 claims "AI-enabled adversary operations increased 89% year-over-year." Run the five questions. CrowdStrike is a vendor that sells endpoint protection (question 3). They do not disclose classification criteria for "AI-enabled" (question 2). Increased detection could reflect better identification, not more attacks (question 2 again). The 89% conflates volume with success rate (question 4). The data is still useful, but not at face value.
