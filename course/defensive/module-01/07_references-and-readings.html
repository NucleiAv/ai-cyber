Academic Papers & Preprints

  • Xu, Z., et al. (2024). "Hallucination is Inevitable: An Innate Limitation of Large Language Models." Paper
    Demonstrates mathematically that hallucination is a structural property of the next-token prediction architecture, not a defect that future model versions will resolve.
    arXiv:2401.11817
  • Spracklen, J., et al. (2025). "We Have a Package for You! A Comprehensive Analysis of Package Hallucinations by Code Generating LLMs." 34th USENIX Security Symposium. Paper
    Analyzed 576,000 code samples from 16 LLMs; found 19.7% of recommended package dependencies were hallucinated, with 43% repeatability enabling supply chain attacks (slopsquatting).
    USENIX Security '25 · arXiv:2406.10279
  • Kuo, M., et al. (2025). "H-CoT: Hijacking the Chain-of-Thought Safety Reasoning Mechanism to Jailbreak Large Reasoning Models, Including OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking." Preprint
    Introduces the Malicious-Educator benchmark, which conceals dangerous requests inside prompts that present themselves as legitimate educational material. The attack supplies text imitating the model's own safety-reasoning phase, so the model proceeds as though it had already completed and passed that check. Cited in Section 4 for the first LRM security implication, injection of attacker-chosen premises into the reasoning chain.
    arXiv:2502.12893
  • Zhao, J., et al. (2025). "Chain-of-Thought Hijacking." Preprint
    Pads a harmful request with long sequences of entirely benign puzzle reasoning and reports attack success rates of 94% to 100% against Gemini 2.5 Pro, o4-mini, Grok 3 mini, and Claude 4 Sonnet on HarmBench. The mechanism the authors identify is refusal dilution: middle layers encode the strength of the safety check and later layers encode its outcome, and a long benign chain attenuates both by drawing attention away from the harmful tokens. Cited in Section 4 for the third LRM security implication, reasoning length as an attack parameter.
    arXiv:2510.26418

Practitioner & Institutional Resources

  • World Economic Forum (WEF). Global Cybersecurity Outlook 2026. Geneva: World Economic Forum, 2026. Report
    Key findings cited: 94% of leaders identify AI as the #1 change driver; 87% identified AI-related vulnerabilities as fastest-growing cyber risk; 64% of organizations assess AI tool security before deployment (up from 37%); "widening cyber inequity."
    WEF Global Cybersecurity Outlook 2026
  • CrowdStrike. 2026 Global Threat Report. CrowdStrike, 2026. Report
    Key findings cited: Average breakout time dropped to 29 minutes (fastest: 27 seconds); 89% year-over-year increase in AI-enabled adversary operations; adversaries injecting malicious prompts into GenAI tools at 90+ organizations.
    CrowdStrike Global Threat Report
  • NCC Group. 2025 Threat Intelligence Report. NCC Group, 2025. Report
    Key finding cited: 124 distinct ransomware groups tracked in 2025, up 50% year over year.
    NCC Group Threat Intelligence
  • SANS Institute. 2025 Detection and Response Survey. SANS Institute, 2025. Report
    Key finding cited: 73% of organizations identify false positives as their #1 detection challenge.
    SANS White Papers
  • UC Berkeley Center for Long-Term Cybersecurity (CLTC). Case study on AI-enabled identity fraud and voice cloning. Research
    Key finding cited: Criminals can clone a person's voice with ~1 hour of YouTube footage and an $11/month subscription; AI-generated identities used to pass remote job interviews.
    UC Berkeley CLTC

Source Caveats

Reading Sources Critically

Every source has a context. When you read security research, keep these caveats in mind:

Source CategoryKey Caveat
Vendor threat reports (CrowdStrike, NCC Group) Built from the vendor's own customer telemetry, so the population is whoever buys that product. Vendors also define terms like “breakout time” and “ransomware group” differently from one another, so cross-vendor and year-over-year comparisons often do not hold. Read the figures as directional.
Survey findings (SANS 2025 Detection and Response, WEF Global Cybersecurity Outlook) Self-reported by respondents who chose to answer. A statistic such as “73% identify false positives as their top challenge” measures what practitioners perceive and report, not what their systems did.
Academic preprints (Kuo et al. 2025, arXiv 2502.12893; Zhao et al. 2025, arXiv 2510.26418) Not peer-reviewed. Cite as a specific finding on a specific date under the conditions the authors describe, and read the methodology before repeating the claim.
Peer-reviewed papers (Xu et al. 2024; Spracklen et al. 2025) Conclusions hold for the models and settings tested. Model behavior shifts between releases, so a measured rate is a snapshot rather than a constant.
Benchmark datasets (UNSW-NB15) Synthetic traffic generated in 2015. Useful for learning the feature vocabulary; not a measure of how any detector performs on current network traffic.
Frameworks (NIST Cybersecurity Framework Profile for AI) A statement of what an organization should do. It is not evidence that doing it works, and it carries no measurement of its own.
