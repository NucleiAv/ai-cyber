The Dataset

Every model on this page will learn from and predict on a real network traffic dataset called . This is a curated subset of 2,000 records sampled from the full UNSW_NB15 dataset, balanced 50:50 between normal traffic and attacks, giving us a manageable playground to experiment with.

Each row represents a single network connection between two machines. The columns capture everything from how long the connection lasted (dur) to how many bytes were sent in each direction (sbytes, dbytes). The final column, label, tells us the ground truth: 0 for normal, 1 for attack.

Rule Based Detection

The simplest approach to threat detection: a human expert writes IF/THEN rules from domain knowledge. No learning from data is involved. The analyst looks at known attack patterns and encodes them as conditions. If any rule matches, the system flags the traffic as malicious.

We have four rules, each targeting a specific suspicious pattern:

  1. High TTL with no response: sttl > 200 AND dpkts == 0
  2. Extreme rate, near zero duration: rate > 100000 AND dur < 0.001
  3. High source load, zero destination load: sload > 10000000 AND dload == 0
  4. State TTL pattern with no response: ct_state_ttl == 2 AND dpkts == 0

The limitation of rules

Look at the recall number above. Rules only catch attacks whose patterns a human explicitly anticipated and wrote down. The rest slip through. This is the fundamental limitation of rule based systems: you can only catch what you can imagine.

Logistic Regression

Instead of hand writing rules, learns a threat score formula from labeled data. Each feature gets a numerical weight. Multiply every feature by its weight, sum them up, add a bias term, and push the total through a to get a probability between 0 and 1.

The beauty of logistic regression is its interpretability. You can look at each feature's contribution to the final score and understand why the model made its decision. Positive contributions push toward "attack," negative contributions push toward "normal."

What the numbers tell us

The ensemble methods (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) generally outperform simpler models. But each model taught a different concept: rules showed the limits of human knowledge encoding, logistic regression revealed feature importance, the decision tree showed interpretable logic, and the ensembles demonstrated the power of combining many learners. In real security operations, the "best" model depends on your priorities: speed, interpretability, accuracy, or some combination of all three.
