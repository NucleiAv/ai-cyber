The Intelligence Cycle Meets the Language Model

Before any AI touches threat intelligence, we need to know what threat intelligence actually is. This section defines the discipline, walks the intelligence cycle, and distinguishes the user types. We'll look at which parts of this process a language model can potentially accelerate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish threat data, threat information, and threat intelligence using the analysis and decision criteria
  • Describe the six phases of the intelligence cycle and assign concrete SOC work items to each phase
  • Differentiate strategic, operational, and tactical intelligence products by their consumers
  • Evaluate which phases of the cycle a language model plausibly accelerates and which require human ownership

Driving Question

When a security team says it "does threat intelligence," what process is actually running, and which parts of that process can a language model legitimately accelerate?

1

Data, Information, Intelligence

You finished Module 4 inside the SOC, evaluating how (well) machines triage alerts. This week we move one desk over, to the analyst whose job it is to understand the adversary. Threat intelligence has a precise meaning that most of the industry's marketing ignores, and the precision starts with three terms that are routinely confused.

  1. 1

    Raw observables, zero context. Four thousand IPs flagged as malicious by an aggregator.

  2. 2

    Data with context added: what, where, when. Thirty of those addresses connected to your VPN gateway since midnight.

  3. 3

    Information analyzed to support a decision. The pattern matches credential stuffing that has preceded ransomware in recent incidents; exposure is confirmed, and the action is to force MFA reenrollment for affected accounts today.

Analysis and decision orientation are what separates intelligence from information: it ends in a judgment a human can act on. NIST SP 800-150, the federal guide to cyber threat information sharing, formalizes this distinction, we will use its vocabulary throughout.

Two more terms belong in our vocabulary before the cycle: the raw components that intelligence is built from. An is the artifact the adversary left behind: a file hash, IP address, domain, or registry key that evidences their presence. A sits above the artifact and describes behavior rather than artifacts: not "this hash," but "this actor delivers ISO archives via phishing and establishes persistence with a scheduled task."

The distinction has real consequences. An adversary can replace a hash or rotate a domain in minutes; indicators are cheap to cycle. Behaviors (the tools, techniques, and operational habits behind those indicators) take much longer to change, because they reflect training, infrastructure investment, and established tradecraft. This asymmetry is why , the field's shared catalog of adversary behaviors, carries so much operational weight; we will study it closer in Module 8. For now, A is the person, group, or organization responsible for the observed activity.

Why this matters

If you cannot describe the intelligence workflow precisely, you cannot evaluate whether an AI actually improved it. This module's central skill is verification of AI-generated intelligence, and that verification requires knowing what a finished product should contain, who it serves, and which step of the cycle produced it. There is also a market reality: "AI-powered threat intelligence" is one of the most aggressively marketed phrases in the industry. A practitioner who can name the process phases can ask the only question that matters about any such product: which phase did you automate, and where did the judgment come from?

2

The Intelligence Cycle

The transformation from data to intelligence runs through a process called the , a model inherited from national security practice and used, with local variations, by every mature team. The cycle is iterative, not a one way conveyor: a finding during analysis routinely sends you back to collection.

  1. 1

    Direction

    Someone decides what questions matter. "Are we exposed to this ransomware campaign?" Intelligence requires a consumer (off-taker).
  2. 2

    Collection

    Gathering raw material: advisories, feeds, vendor reports, internal telemetry.
  3. 3

    Processing

    Making collected material usable: deduplication, normalization, translation, formatting.
  4. 4

    Analysis

    The judgment step. Evaluating evidence, weighing source reliability, connecting observations to actor behavior, assessing what it means for the organization.
  5. 5

    Dissemination

    Delivering the product to the 'consumer' in a form they can use: a briefing, a blocklist, a patch priority list.
  6. 6

    Feedback

    The 'consumer' tells us whether the product answered the question, which redirects the next cycle.

There is one more distinction to carry through the module. Intelligence products serve three consumer levels: . The same raw advisory becomes three different products depending on who asked, and understanding which product we are producing shapes everything about format and depth.

Let's walk the cycle with a concrete object. A new CISA advisory describes a ransomware campaign. Direction: Our asks whether the organization is exposed. Collection: we pull the advisory, the vendor writeups it references, and your own asset inventory. Processing: we extract the affected product list and the indicators into a normalized table. Analysis: we compare affected versions against your inventory, weigh whether the initial access technique applies to your environment, and form a judgment. Dissemination: a one-page exposure assessment to the CISO, a blocklist to the . Feedback: the CISO says the assessment answered the question but arrived a day late, so we adjust.

The asymmetry to remember

In that walkthrough, every phase except one moved or reshaped text. Analysis compared external claims against internal reality and produced a judgment. Keep that asymmetry in mind for the rest of the module: language models are text engines, as Module 4 established, and they are strong precisely where the work is moving and reshaping text. The cycle maps directly to that boundary.

3

One Question, Three Customers

Fictional scenario, modeled on routine advisory handling. A ransomware advisory for a widely used file transfer product is published Monday morning. Three people ask for help before lunch... Same advisory, three products, three different mixes of cycle phases.

The CISO asks

"Are we exposed, and do I need to tell the board anything?" The product is strategic: two paragraphs, plain language, an exposure judgment, no indicators, no jargon. Production cost concentrates in analysis (advisory against asset inventory) and dissemination (writing for a non-technical reader).

The SOC lead asks

"What do we block and hunt for?" The product is tactical: a normalized indicator table and a hunting note describing the actor's post-access behavior. Production cost concentrates in processing (extraction, deduplication, defanging) with a thin analysis layer (which indicators are current and relevant).

The vulnerability manager asks

"Which of our systems patch first?" The product is tactical and operational: the affected version list joined against the inventory, ranked by exposure. Production cost is processing plus a judgment about priority.

When we get to AI-assisted summarization, we will see that the model's usefulness differs across these three products for a reason we can now name: they demand different ratios of text transformation to judgment.

Watch out: three common misconceptions

"More feeds means better intelligence." Volume is data. Unconsumed feeds are cost, not capability. Direction, not collection, is where intelligence quality starts.

"Intelligence is a deliverable format." A polished PDF that ends in no decision is information at best, and a wasteful paperweight otherwise. The decision orientation is the definition.

"AI does threat intelligence now." A model can draft, summarize, extract, and reformat, exactly the pattern Module 4 mapped for AI-assisted security tasks. The phases that own questions and judgments (direction, analysis conclusions, feedback) require human accountability for the same reasons Module 4 identified; The verification methodology later in the module applies that principle specifically to CTI.

The intelligence cycle is a model, and real teams sometimes risk blurring its phases; a small team runs the whole cycle in one analyst's head before coffee. Critics inside the intelligence community have argued that the cycle idealizes a messier, more parallel reality. It does still remain the shared vocabulary of the discipline, and this course uses it as a map, not a law. Note also what this section deliberately defers, we're not touching on the adversary yet. The adversary's perspective on these same tools belongs to Module 8, and depth belongs to the treatment there.

Part 2 · Decide what you would delegate

Applied analysis: delegation judgment

For each of the six work items from Part 1, mark it: AI delegable today, AI assisted with human review, or human only. Write one sentence of justification per item in your course notes. There is no single right mapping; your justification is what counts. Hint: ask how much of each item is moving and reshaping text versus producing a judgment someone must own. Record your answers; you will revisit them when we reach the verification methodology, where the module gives you a principled framework for exactly this call.

Use these questions to pressure-test each call:

  • Text ratio: Is the output primarily reorganized or reformatted text, or does it require comparing claims against context the model has never seen?
  • Ownership: Does a person need to sign this output and be accountable for it next quarter?
  • Irreversibility: Could acting on this output without human review cause a cost that cannot be unspent?
  • Access: Does producing a correct answer require your organization's internal data (asset inventory, telemetry, prior incidents)?

The verification methodology formalizes these as four named properties. Compare your intuitive calls now against the framework then.

Check Your Understanding

Knowledge Check 1

Your team receives three artifacts: (1) a feed of 4,000 IP addresses flagged as malicious by an aggregator; (2) a note that 30 of those IPs connected to your VPN gateway since midnight; (3) an assessment that the connection pattern matches credential stuffing preceding ransomware deployment in recent incidents, with a recommendation to force MFA reenrollment. Which is intelligence?

The Pyramid of Pain. A widely used community heuristic (David Bianco, 2013) ranking indicator types by how much it costs the adversary when you act on them: hash values are trivial to change, IPs cheap, domains moderate, and TTPs expensive. It is a practitioner heuristic, not a standard, but it explains why TTP-level intelligence is prized and why blocklists of hashes decay so fast.

History of the cycle. The intelligence cycle predates cybersecurity by decades; reading critiques of it from the national security literature is a good way to understand both its value as shared vocabulary and its limits as a description of real work.
