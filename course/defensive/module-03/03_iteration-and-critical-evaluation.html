Systematic Iteration & Critical Evaluation

How to improve a security prompt in a way you can defend with evidence, and how to catch the confident, fluent, completely fabricated output before it reaches a decision.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • Iterate on a prompt against a held-out test set, measuring accuracy and run-to-run consistency and changing one variable at a time.
  • Document each prompt version, its measured effect, and your keep-or-discard decision.
  • Evaluate outputs critically, detecting and verifying hallucinated findings against authoritative sources before acting.
1

From Impression to Measurement

Sections 1 and 2 leave you with a structured prompt and a clear understanding that you cannot trust a single run. This section covers the two things that resolve that problem: a disciplined iteration process, and a verification habit that will define the rest of the course.

The iteration process is called systematic iteration: improve the prompt against a labeled , measure both and run-to-run , change one thing at a time, and write down what you changed and why.

The verification habit is critical evaluation: treat every model output as a draft to be verified, not an answer to be trusted. Language models routinely produce fluent, specific, and wrong security claims, including fabricated vulnerability identifiers, invented severity scores, and nonexistent software packages.

2

The Iteration Loop

Improving a prompt is an experiment, and experiments have rules. Here is a loop that enforces both systematic iteration and critical evaluation:

  1. 1

    Fix a held-out test set

    A small, representative sample of labeled security items (log lines, alerts, or whatever artifacts your task involves) with known answers. The exact size depends on your use case; 10 to 20 items is a reasonable starting point for many tasks, but some may need more.

  2. 2

    Run the current prompt

    Across all items; record accuracy. Run a few items several times; record a consistency rate.

  3. 3

    Change exactly one variable

    Add an example, fix the order, tighten the output contract, or lower the temperature.

  4. 4

    Re-run and re-measure

    Same test set, same procedure.

  5. 5

    Record it in the change log

    With a keep-or-discard decision.

  6. 6

    Repeat

    Until improvement plateaus.

Two principles help make this process more reliable.

One variable at a time

If you change three things and accuracy rises, you do not know which change helped. You have learned nothing transferable, and you cannot reproduce the improvement next time.

Measure across runs, not once

Output is stochastic (Section 2), so a single run is a single sample. Confirm a gain by seeing it hold across multiple runs. Repetition changes the answer, so repetition must be part of how you measure.

There is evidence behind the second principle: self-consistency research showed that aggregating multiple sampled answers and taking the majority improved performance on a reasoning benchmark by about 18 points over a single answer. The exact number varies by model and task, but the lesson holds broadly.

What a real iteration record looks like

The below tracks an alert-triage prompt across five versions. The numbers are illustrative on a 15-item labeled set; yours will differ. Read each step as: the single change, the resulting accuracy, the consistency across reruns, and the keep-or-discard decision. Consistency is noted as, for example, "3/3, 2/3, 3/3", meaning three test items were each run three times. The first and third items gave the same answer all three times; the second agreed on only two of three.

  1. 1

    v1: baseline structured prompt (from Section 1)

    Accuracy 9 of 15. Consistency 3/3, 2/3, 3/3. Decision: keep as the baseline.
  2. 2

    v2: add 2 curated few-shot examples, including one boundary case

    Accuracy 12 of 15. Consistency 3/3, 3/3, 3/3. Decision: keep. Few-shot helped.
  3. 3

    v3: reorder examples so the boundary case is last

    Accuracy 11 of 15. Consistency 3/3, 2/3, 3/3. Decision: discard. This ordering was worse.
  4. 4

    v4: tighten the output contract to a fixed label set

    Accuracy 12 of 15. Consistency 3/3, 3/3, 3/3. Decision: keep. Same accuracy, better parseability.
  5. 5

    v5: lower temperature from 0.7 to 0

    Accuracy 12 of 15. Consistency now identical across reruns. Decision: keep for repeatability.

The log tells a story a reviewer can follow: few-shot helped (v2), that particular reordering hurt (v3, discarded), the tighter contract held accuracy while improving parseability (v4), and dropping temperature bought repeatability without costing accuracy (v5). Nobody has to take your word that the prompt is good. The evidence is on the page.

Why Models Fabricate (and Why It Matters)

A well-iterated prompt still produces , and in security those hallucinations are not harmless. They are exploitable or decision-corrupting.

The reason hallucination is structural, not a bug awaiting a patch, traces back to what you learned in Module 2: the model generates each token by predicting what is most probable given everything before it. It does not look up facts in a database. A CVE number that looks right (correct format, plausible year, realistic severity) is exactly the kind of token sequence the model was trained to produce. Fabrication and generation are the same process.

  • Fabricated identifiers. Models invent numbers and scores that look real. A 2025 study found that scoring vulnerabilities from a description is inherently challenging and that models systematically misclassify, so a confident CVSS vector from a model can be simply wrong.
  • Hallucinated packages that become real attacks ("slopsquatting"). A 2025 study at the USENIX Security Symposium found that code-generating models recommend nonexistent software packages at rates from about 5 percent (commercial models) to about 22 percent (open models), and that 58 percent of hallucinated names recur across queries. Attackers register the predictable fake name with malicious code; one such name was downloaded over 30,000 times. A reliability flaw became a supply-chain attack vector.
  • Knowledge-cutoff blind spots. Ask about a vulnerability published after the model's training cutoff and the model will often produce a confident, fictional description rather than admit it does not know.

The professional response is a verification habit: every atomic, checkable claim in model output must be cross-checked against an authoritative source before that output informs a decision. Check CVE identifiers and CVSS scores against the , package names against the actual package registry, and IP addresses and attributions against your own ground truth.

Claim-by-claim verification in practice

Here is a model-produced triage note for an alert that referenced a failed-authentication burst. On first reading, it looks thorough and actionable:

"This activity matches CVE-2026-99999, a critical SSH authentication-bypass
vulnerability (CVSS 9.8) actively exploited since January 2026. Recommend
immediate patching per vendor advisory."

The note is fluent, specific, and authoritative-sounding. But that is exactly why it is dangerous. Almost every claim in it is unverifiable as written, and at least one does not hold up.

  • The identifier CVE-2026-99999. Search the NVD for it. There is no such record. A CVE identifier that does not appear in the NVD is a fabrication. Stop here: the whole note is now suspect.
  • The score CVSS 9.8. A score is meaningless without a real CVE to attach it to, and even for real CVEs, models miscompute CVSS. Never propagate a model-produced score without checking the authoritative record.
  • "Actively exploited since January 2026." An exploitation claim must be checked against an authoritative source such as the catalog, not accepted because it sounds urgent.
  • "Per vendor advisory." Which advisory? There is no link and no identifier. An unverifiable citation is not a citation.

Fluency is not truth

The note's fluency and specificity are what make it persuasive, and they have no correlation with truth. A single fabricated identifier, acted on, wastes an emergency patch cycle or, worse, distracts from the real cause of the alert. Your job is not to be impressed by how confident the output sounds. Your job is to verify each claim.

4

Putting Verification into Practice

Here are the misconceptions that most commonly undermine iteration and verification work. Each shows up again in later modules.

Common misconceptions

  • "It looks better, so it's better." Without a measured comparison on a test set across runs, "looks better" is not evidence. The change log replaces impressions with measurements.
  • "I improved accuracy by changing the prompt," said after changing several things at once. You cannot attribute the gain to any one change, and next time you will not be able to reproduce the improvement.
  • "Confident and specific means correct." This is the single most dangerous assumption in applied AI security. Fluency is easy for the model to produce; truth must be checked by you.
  • "Better prompting will stop hallucinations." Better prompting lowers their frequency. It does not remove the need to verify.

Tradeoffs to keep in mind

  • Small test sets give noisy estimates. A 10-item set is enough to practice the method and catch gross problems, but a 2-item difference may be noise. State your set size and treat small gaps cautiously.
  • Verification has a cost. Checking every claim against the NVD or CISA KEV takes time. In a high-volume you cannot manually verify everything. That is an argument for designing prompts that output their sources and for building automation that flags unverifiable claims, not for skipping verification.
  • Reliability ceilings are real. For some tasks, such as scoring CVSS from a description, no amount of prompt iteration reaches reliable accuracy. Recognizing a ceiling and stating it clearly is part of being a professional. Section 4 covers how to approach production-readiness decisions with this reality in mind.

Try it yourself: critique a change-log entry

You inherit a triage prompt with this change-log entry: "v6: added 3 examples, reordered all examples, switched to output, raised temperature to 0.9. Accuracy 13 of 15, up from 10 of 15. Kept." In four or five sentences, identify at least two methodological problems with this entry, state what you cannot conclude from it, describe the minimal re-runs needed to find out which change actually mattered, and explain why the temperature choice is questionable for a production triage prompt.

  • Multiple variables changed at once, so the gain from 10 to 13 cannot be attributed to any single change.
  • The single accuracy number lacks a consistency measurement and may be noise on a 15-item set.
  • Raising temperature to 0.9 increases run-to-run variability, which is the opposite of what a repeatable production prompt needs.
  • To identify which change mattered: revert to v5 and reintroduce each change one at a time, measuring accuracy and consistency across several runs per version, ideally at temperature 0 for the comparison.
Knowledge Check

A model's alert summary states that the alert corresponds to CVE-2026-12345 with a CVSS of 9.1. The output is well-formatted and confident. What is your next action?

Swap prompts with a classmate and try to make each other's prompt fail on edge cases or ambiguous inputs. Document the breaks. This is a controlled preview of the adversarial mindset you will apply in Module 9.
