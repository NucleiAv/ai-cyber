Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • Locate any concept from the course by the module it was covered in and the work that went with it.
  • Identify which of that work you could still walk someone through, and which you would need to revisit first.

The course covered a lot of ground across thirteen weeks, and it is easy to lose track of what was where. This section collects it in one place: what each module covered, and what you worked through alongside it.

Read it as a record, and use it however it fits. Some readers will be assembling something to show an employer, in which case Section 4's evidence rule applies and this is the list it runs against. Others are already working in security and will be looking for the piece they now need at work. The list serves both, and either way it is worth thinking of what follows as a repertoire you can draw on rather than a set of completed assignments.

1

How to Work Through This

Go through the tables once and mark each row one of three ways.

  • Could walk someone through it. You remember the method, not only the conclusion, and you could explain why you made the calls you made.
  • Would need to revisit it. You did the work, but the reasoning has faded, or you kept no record of it.
  • Did not get to it. The module page is still there, and the ones you skipped are the shortest route to filling a gap you know you have.

The middle case is the common one. Most of these can be reconstructed in an evening from the module page and your own notes, and it is easier to do while the reasoning is still recent. A note written now records what you decided and why; one written a year from now records what you remember deciding.

Before you share any of it

Section 4 covered what can and cannot be handed to someone outside the course, including the constraint that NYU treats course material as university property, so what you may share depends on your program's terms. That applies to everything on this page. The method, scope, and outcome of a piece of work can usually be described even where the work itself cannot be produced.

Where the Module Material Stops

The module material has edges, and they are worth stating. Most of it was worked on curated data, at your own pace, without an environment moving underneath it. The course projects push two of those edges further than the module pages do, and they are noted below. The rest stay where they are.

  • Detection tuning at production scale. The module labs used prepared datasets, chosen so the signal would be findable. Live traffic behaves differently, and the course projects are where that shows up: alert volumes, drift, and outputs that move while you are working on them. It is still a simulation. Running the same tuning against a real environment, over months, with the operational cost of every threshold change landing on someone, is the part that stays outside a term.
  • Live incident response. The incident work in the modules was tabletop, and paced by you. The course projects add time pressure and a situation that changes as you work, which is closer, though still simulated. What no course can supply is a real system degrading, with real consequences to the decisions and no option to reread the page.
  • Longitudinal operation. Every assessment was a snapshot. How a control behaves after a year, as models are updated and attackers adapt, is not something a term can show.
  • Organizational consequence. The governance work assessed fictional organizations. No recommendation had to be argued for or funded.

These are the gaps that working in the field fills, and Section 4's seven paths are the routes toward them. For anyone already working in security, they are also the points where this material meets conditions no course reproduces in full.

6

Keeping a Usable Record

A row in a table is a reminder, not a record. What makes any of this usable later is a few sentences per item, written in the same three-part shape Section 4 used for work that cannot be shared directly.

  1. 1

    Method

    What you did and against what standard. "Assessed a patient-facing RAG assistant against all ten OWASP LLM categories, grading each control present, partial, or absent." This is the part that survives confidentiality constraints, and the part you will want when you next run the same method on something real.

  2. 2

    Scope

    What was in and out: the size of the dataset, the number of categories, the fact that the environment was simulated. Stating the scope is what stops a reader, including you later, from over-reading the result.

  3. 3

    Outcome and what you would change

    What you found, and what you would do differently with the work in front of you again. The second half tends to be where the learning actually sits.

Three or four sentences per item is enough. Kept together in one file, the result is something you can scan when you need the method again, whether that is for a conversation about a role or for the week you are asked to assess a deployment at work.
