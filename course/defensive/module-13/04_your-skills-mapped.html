1

Evidence, Not Aspiration

Security job postings increasingly list AI-related skills: detection evaluation, prompt engineering, governance, agent oversight. By module 13, the question is not whether you have exposure to those topics, but whether you can point to something specific you produced in this course that demonstrates each one.

The rule is simple: a skill claim requires a nameable . Not "I am familiar with detection metrics," but "here is the confusion matrix I computed on real intrusion-detection output, and here is the operational extrapolation I drew from it."

Where no exists, the skill is a gap. That is the point, not a shortcoming: gaps are what a learning plan is built from, and a plan without them is guesswork.

When you cannot share the artifact

In practice, many security artifacts are proprietary, classified, or covered by NDA. A vendor evaluation you performed for an employer, a gap assessment containing internal risk data, or a threat-intelligence report with sensitive indicators cannot be handed to an interviewer. That does not make the skill unevidenceable.

The professional version of the evidence rule works in layers. You describe the method you used (the type of analysis, the framework, the evaluation criteria), the scope of the work (how many systems, what kind of environment), and the outcome in general terms, without revealing protected details. Some practitioners keep a sanitized portfolio: the same analysis rerun on public data, or a write-up that replaces proprietary specifics with anonymized equivalents.

Course work has its own constraints: NYU considers course material university property, so sharing it depends on the specific terms of your program. Regardless of what you can or cannot hand over, the habit of documenting your method (not just your results) is what keeps the evidence rule usable across your career. An interviewer who hears a clear description of what you did, how you scoped it, and what you learned can assess the skill whether or not they see the original output.

The scaffolding for this already exists. The breaks roles into task, knowledge, and skill statements, most recently updated in spring 2026, with an interactive career-pathways tool built on top of it. The matrix below borrows its decomposition logic, and the optional links in the references let you explore the full framework.

One data point for context: ISC2 publishes an annual workforce survey (typically over ten thousand respondents) that has consistently ranked AI among the top skill needs, and increasingly frames the profession's core problem as a skills gap rather than a headcount gap.

That finding should be weighed appropriately: the data is self-reported, and ISC2 sells certifications.

What matters is narrower than the headline: the evaluation and constraint skills this course practiced are scarce, and scarce skills are where early careers tend to find traction.

If You Are Working Outside the United States

The workforce framework behind the matrix below, and the role titles used with it, are American. That is where the most detailed public role decomposition happens to be published, not a judgment that it is the right one to use. The mapping method holds anywhere. The labels travel less well: SOC analyst and GRC analyst are recognizable in many markets and titled differently in others, and the expectation that a candidate arrives with a portfolio of work is a hiring convention in some places rather than a rule everywhere.

The nearest counterpart outside the United States is the European Cybersecurity Skills Framework, published by ENISA in 2022, which decomposes twelve role profiles into missions, tasks, skills, and knowledge in much the way the matrix here does. Several countries maintain their own national schemes alongside it. Build the map against whichever framework is actually cited where you are: the one your prospective employers reference if you are looking for a role, or the one your organization already uses if you are in one. It is also worth knowing whether demonstrated work, a certification, or a formal qualification carries the most weight in that market, since the three are not weighted the same everywhere.

What transfers without translation is the underlying habit: naming the artifact, describing the method that produced it, and being able to talk through both. That holds whether the map is for an application or for a conversation with your own manager, and whatever the local vocabulary turns out to be.

Three ways the map goes wrong

Rating interest as capability. "I find threat intelligence fascinating" and "I produced a validated intelligence assessment with graded sources" are different claims. Both may prompt follow-up questions, but only the second gives the interviewer something concrete to ask about.

Treating gaps as failures. The gap column is where your plan comes from. A map filled in without any is a map you cannot use.

Collecting certifications instead of capabilities. A certification tells a hiring manager somebody else assessed you. An lets them assess you themselves, which many of them prefer.

Reasoning Task: Read the Posting

Map the requirements

A posting for a junior SOC analyst lists these AI-related requirements:

  1. Experience evaluating ML-based detection systems (precision, recall, operational cost)
  2. Familiarity with LLM risk taxonomies and failure modes
  3. Ability to assess vendor AI claims against evidence
  4. Participation in AI governance or policy-drafting processes
  5. Hands-on MLOps or production ML deployment experience

Map each requirement to course work that demonstrates it, and identify the one requirement no course covers.

Four requirements map to specific labs and exercises: detection evaluation, LLM risk taxonomies, vendor-claim assessment, and governance participation. The fifth, typically production MLOps or deployment experience, has no artifact and belongs in a learning plan. Finding the gap is the point, not a shortfall.

    Where to Go Next: Seven Paths

    Each path below connects to course work you can name. None is a prerequisite for, or a preview of, any specific course.

    Builds on: the attack-taxonomy work, where you classified attacks across three lenses rather than memorizing one list. Where to start: the national adversarial-ML taxonomy and the adversarial tactics knowledge base you already used for classification. Work forward from techniques you can already name, and read the primary papers behind two or three of them rather than skimming all of them.

    Builds on: the injection analysis and the jailbreak material alongside it. Where to start: published red-team methodologies, then hands-on practice through prompt-injection capture-the-flag exercises, which exist in public and are free. The skill that distinguishes a red teamer from an enthusiast is documentation, so write up every finding as though someone has to remediate it.

    Builds on: the deployment-hardening work. This is also the gap the SOC analyst example row surfaced, which makes it a natural first choice. Where to start: the control-overlay project for AI systems as it publishes, plus the deployment-security guidance already sitting in your references. A direct way to close this gap is to run something small in production yourself and watch what breaks.

    Builds on: the triage pipeline from the detection module, and the rule-drafting work that followed. Where to start: open detection-rule repositories and, more importantly, their test harnesses. Reading rules teaches you syntax; running them against labeled traffic teaches you why most rules are wrong the first time.

    Builds on: the gap assessment you ran in the governance module, which is a genuine portfolio and one most applicants do not have. Where to start: the risk-management framework's resource center and its published profile documents, which show how other organizations answered the same questions you answered for NorthShore Mutual.

    Builds on: the failure-mode catalog from Section 2 and the calibration discipline from Section 3. Where to start: publications from the major labs, read with exactly the sourcing skepticism Section 3 developed. A lab publishing on the safety of systems it also sells is a source with unique visibility and a commercial position at the same time, which is exactly the pattern the sourcing exercises practiced.

    Builds on: this module directly, and on the agent you built in the design studio. Where to start: the practitioner pattern literature in the references, then build a small agent yourself, give it two or three real tools, and constrain it properly. You will understand the failure catalog differently once one of its entries has happened to something you wrote.

    Decision: The Containment Authority Call

    Your triage agent has run in report-only mode for six months with 94% precision on its practice distribution. The CISO proposes granting it authority to auto-isolate hosts it classifies as actively compromised, arguing mean-time-to-contain would drop from 41 minutes to under two.

    You are the security engineering lead. Make the call, then read what your call commits you to. All three are defensible, and each one has a bill attached.

    Where the argument actually lives

    Two words in that 94% figure matter. It describes a practice distribution, and it is an aggregate. Each hides something you have learned to look for: the , and what an aggregate metric conceals until you disaggregate it.

    Then consider the action itself. Host isolation sits in a particular class, and reversibility is what decides where a belongs.

    Positions Revisited, and What You Carry

    Here are the three positions you committed to at the start of the module, with what the evidence did to each one.

    A. An agent that performs well in a demo will behave the same way in production.

    B. An agent whose tools are all read-only cannot cause a security incident.

    C. The main risk of deploying an autonomous agent is that an attacker hacks it.

    Look back at which of your three positions moved the furthest. The position that shifted is often the one where you were reasoning from intuition rather than from mechanism, and knowing that about yourself is worth more than any single fact here.

    Before reading the closing paragraph, try this: name one thing about that this module deliberately left unresolved, and say what the actual obstacle to resolving it is. If the obstacle you land on is "nobody has measured it yet," you have understood where the field stands.

    Examples you might have landed on

    Whether agentic triage actually reduces analyst fatigue or just shifts it from reading alerts to reviewing agent output (answering this would require across different SOC sizes, sectors, and alert volumes, and those studies take years to run). Whether human-in-the-loop gates degrade into rubber-stamping under real alert volumes (several teams suspect they do, but measuring it requires instrumentation most SOCs do not yet have in place). What false-positive rate justifies letting an agent contain a host without human approval (operational consensus is hard to reach here because the acceptable rate depends on the sector, the asset criticality, and the cost of a missed containment). Whether specification gaming gets worse or better as models scale (tracking this requires comparable benchmarks across model generations, which are difficult to design and expensive to maintain).

    Each of these is a question the module surfaced but could not answer, because the evidence that would answer it is genuinely hard to produce.

    This course began with a machine that could not reliably tell you what it knew. It ends with machines that act on their own account.

    What sits between those two points is not a body of facts about particular products, most of which will be obsolete before you need them. It is a set of habits. Compute the matrix before trusting the detector. Verify the claim before repeating it. Name the owner before accepting the risk. Read the trace before believing the story.

    The tools will change underneath you, and they will change often. The habits are the part that stays with you.
