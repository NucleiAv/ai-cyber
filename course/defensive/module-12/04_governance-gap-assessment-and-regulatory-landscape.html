Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • Evaluate an AI security deployment for governance gaps by applying all four functions as a structured checklist, producing evidence-tied findings and named-artifact recommendations.
  • Assess the relevance of current AI regulation to a described deployment using the locate-classify-map-datestamp method, without relying on memorized regulatory detail.
  • Justify a committed position on an unsettled dual-use disclosure question, structured by governance criteria rather than intuition.

The Scenario: NorthShore Mutual

Here is the full NorthShore Mutual brief. Treat it as the complete record of what an assessor could learn in a first week on site; everything the assessment needs is here.

NorthShore Mutual, a mid-size financial services firm, runs a 14-person . Its AI footprint, as of this quarter:

  • An LLM triage assistant in production for eight months, evaluated once at launch on aggregate metrics and never re-evaluated.
  • An agentic remediation pilot with write access to the platform, deployed by an enthusiastic engineer, with no pre-deployment review and no named business owner.
  • A vendor GenAI add-on bought last quarter through standard software procurement, with no AI-specific questions asked.
  • No AI acceptable-use policy, and analysts have been observed pasting alert payloads into personal chatbot accounts.
  • Comprehensive logging, with no AI-specific detections, no AI incident procedure, and no designated rollback authority.
  • A (chief information security officer) whose stated goal is "AI everywhere by Q3."
  • A subsidiary in Frankfurt, Germany, that shares the tooling.

While reading the brief, think like an assessor: each sentence is either evidence of a control, evidence of a gap, or irrelevant detail.

The Assessment, Demonstrated on One Function

The assessment method runs each function through four moves: guiding questions, evidence hunt, findings graded present/partial/absent, and recommendations that name artifacts. Here is a walkthrough of Govern as a , in the same format the next activity uses.

  1. 1

    Guiding questions

    Who has authority to approve AI deployments, against what criteria? What policies govern AI use? Who is accountable for each system?

  2. 2

    Evidence hunt

    Here is what the brief yields: the agent pilot went live with no review and has no named owner. The GenAI add-on passed through procurement with no AI-specific evaluation. No acceptable-use policy exists, and shadow AI is already observable. The CISO's Q3 goal is a stated intention with no decision structure behind it.

  3. 3

    Findings

    (1) Deployment approval authority: absent. Evidence: the agent pilot deployed on one engineer's initiative; no review record exists for any of the three systems. (2) AI use policy: absent. Evidence: none exists; personal-chatbot use is occurring unmanaged. (3) System ownership: partial. Evidence: the triage assistant sits inside SOC operations with implicit ownership; the agent and the add-on have none.

  4. 4

    Recommendations

    For (1): a pre-deployment review gate, owned by a named security architecture function, with written criteria covering permissions, blast radius, and data exposure. The agent pilot goes through it retroactively, first. For (2): the acceptable-use policy drafted in Section 3, adopted with the enforcement mechanisms its clauses name. For (3): an ownership register where every AI system gets a named business owner or gets decommissioned.

Every finding ties to a specific sentence in the brief, and every recommendation names an artifact with an owner. None of them say "improve governance." That is the standard for the next activity too.

That walkthrough only covers Govern. Map, Measure, and Manage are next, and two of the scenario's most consequential gaps live in those three functions.

Activity 3: what the other checklist cannot see

The Distinction Question

Pick one finding from this assessment that the application-security checklist we used previously could not have produced, and settle on why before revealing. What does that difference reveal about the organizational layer?

The application-security checklist would find the chatbot-layer gaps: missing input validation, no output filtering on the triage assistant, no rate limits on the add-on. What it structurally cannot find is "no person had authority to approve the agent's write access," because no technical category asks who decides. That is the organizational layer: the OWASP assessment interrogates the system, while the RMF assessment interrogates the organization around the system. NorthShore's most dangerous gap falls in the second category, and a technical checklist alone would never have found it.

Possible Complete Model Assessment (open after the three activities)

The full findings-and-recommendations record for Map, Measure, and Manage, in the same format as the Govern demonstration. For every finding, ask: did the activities above surface this? If not, which lens did I skip?

Map. (1) System context and failure-consequence analysis: absent. Evidence: the add-on passed procurement "with no AI-specific questions asked"; the agent went live with no review; no document answers "what happens when each system is wrong?" Recommendation: a one-page context record per AI system (purpose, decision consumers, failure consequences, affected parties), owned by each system's business owner, re-reviewed on workflow change. (2) Regulatory exposure mapping: absent. Evidence: "a Frankfurt subsidiary that shares the tooling," and nothing in the brief shows anyone has classified the deployments under EU rules. Recommendation: a deployment-classification memo per system, owned by the compliance function, date-stamped, using the method taught later in this section.

Measure. (1) Ongoing and disaggregated evaluation: absent. Evidence: "evaluated once at launch on aggregate metrics and never re-evaluated." Both problems matter: no re-evaluation means drift goes undetected, and aggregate-only evaluation means the kind of disparity the lab demonstrated could be present and hidden. Recommendation: a quarterly evaluation report, disaggregated by business unit, with a named review owner. (2) Metrics for agent actions: absent. Evidence: the write-capable agent has no stated success, error, or rollback-frequency metrics anywhere in the brief. Recommendation: an agent action ledger with error-rate reporting, owned by the SOC lead.

Manage. (1) AI incident procedure and rollback authority: absent. Evidence: "no AI incident procedure, no designated rollback authority" despite comprehensive logging, which means they can detect problems but have no process for responding to them. Recommendation: an AI incident runbook with severity tiers, an escalation owner, and named rollback authority for each system, exercised in a tabletop before the next deployment. (2) Risk-ranked treatment plan: absent. Evidence: "AI everywhere by Q3" is an intention, not a plan; nothing prioritizes which risks get treated first. Recommendation: a risk register for the three existing systems, reviewed monthly, gating the Q3 expansion.

Two gaps that depend on earlier material. If the aggregate-only evaluation (Measure) or the ungoverned write-capable agent (Govern/Map) did not come up during the activities, revisit the disaggregation lab and the agency discussion from earlier in the module. Both of those gaps are hard to spot without the concepts we built there.

The Regulatory Landscape, as of July 2026

Dated Content, and a Disclaimer

Everything in this block is stated as of July 2026 and is expected to change. No assessment in this course tests a regulatory date. This is method instruction, not legal advice.

Between late 2023 and mid-2026, the United States enacted and then rescinded a comprehensive AI risk-management order, and the European Union adopted a major AI law and then deferred parts of its own timeline. Regulations matter for deployment decisions, but they change faster than most people expect. The durable skill is knowing how to read them, not memorizing their current state.

The EU's approach: risk tiers by use context

The , in force since August 2024, classifies AI systems by what they are used for, not what architecture they use.

TierLogicSecurity-relevant example
Prohibited Practices the Act bans outright regardless of safeguards Untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, the practice at the center of the enforcement case from earlier in this module
High-risk Uses with significant potential impact; triggers the full obligation chain An AI system operating as a safety component in the management of critical digital infrastructure
Transparency-required Systems whose users must know they are interacting with AI or seeing generated content A customer-facing security chatbot; AI-generated incident summaries shared externally
Minimal Everything else; no new obligations An internal spell-checker or code-completion aid with no consequential decisions attached

classification triggers a set of obligations: risk management, data governance, human oversight (Article 14, assigned as reading before the next part), and accuracy and cybersecurity requirements. Whether a security AI deployment lands in a high-risk category is a classification argument, the kind of move the Map function trains, and it turns on intended use, deployment context, and what happens downstream when the system errs. General-purpose model obligations took effect in August 2025, and a simplification package agreed in June 2026 deferred the high-risk compliance dates into 2027 and 2028. Those dates are stated here to be checked, not memorized.

The US approach, currently: acceleration

The October 2023 executive order that built federal AI governance around risk management was rescinded in January 2025. The replacement shifted direction: a new order, follow-on memoranda, and a national action plan focused on removing regulatory barriers and accelerating adoption, with federal AI security workstreams covering threat-information sharing and AI incident response capacity. A deployment decision made in 2024 under one federal posture faced a different posture in 2026. Organizations that had built their governance around risk management rather than compliance with one specific rule set absorbed the change without rebuilding. The rules changed, but the underlying risks had not.

The method: locate, classify, map, date-stamp

  1. 1

    Locate

    Find the current text and status of the regulation, including amendments. Never trust a summary older than the last news cycle, and that includes this one.

  2. 2

    Classify

    Place the deployment under the regulation's own logic: use context, affected parties, consequence of error.

  3. 3

    Map

    Identify the obligations that classification triggers and attach each to an owner and an artifact. This is the gap-assessment move again, just with legal categories. Note that exist independently of anything the vendor claims.

  4. 4

    Date-stamp

    State the conclusion as "as of [date], based on [text version]." Every analysis has a shelf life, and treating it as permanent is how outdated compliance memos become liabilities.

Brief note on Certification and standards!

An international standard for AI management systems now exists, and vendors increasingly advertise certification against it. Read those claims carefully: certification means a management process exists and was audited. It does not mean any particular system is safe, just as a guardrail's presence did not mean a chatbot was secure. That is the distinction. In a procurement conversation, certification tells us about process maturity. It is a starting point for asking better questions, not a substitute for them.

Classify Without Memorizing

Required Reading Before This Task

Read Article 14 (Human Oversight) of the EU AI Act before continuing. It is about a page and takes roughly 10 minutes. As we read, pay attention to which oversight requirements assume the human operator can actually override or stop the system, and think about how that sits with the automation-bias evidence we covered earlier in this module.

Reasoning Task: The Frankfurt Question

Use the regulatory landscape we covered in Section 4 (the EU AI Act tiers, the US policy reversal, and the locate-classify-map-datestamp method) along with Article 14. Keep the four-step method in mind: locate the relevant text, classify by use context, map obligations to owners, and date-stamp the conclusion.

  1. Does NorthShore's agentic remediation pilot, as used by the Frankfurt subsidiary, plausibly fall in an EU high-risk category? Point to the specific classification logic or high-risk list entry that supports the argument, and identify the one additional fact about the deployment that would most change the answer.
  2. What date-stamp caveat does this analysis need?
  3. Which RMF function does this entire exercise fall under? Be ready to explain why.

(1) Two classifications are defensible, and we are not picking one here on purpose. The argument turns on whether the EDR estate the agent can modify qualifies as management of critical digital infrastructure, and whether the agent acts as a safety component in that management. If NorthShore's infrastructure is ordinary corporate IT, the high-risk hook weakens; if the Frankfurt subsidiary processes payments or operates regulated financial infrastructure, it strengthens. A good answer argues from use context either way and names the flip-fact: what the EDR estate actually protects.

(2) A workable caveat: "This classification is based on the consolidated Act text as amended through June 2026, accessed July 2026; the high-risk timeline and category definitions are under active revision and this analysis expires with them."

(3) Map. Classify the system by intended use, affected parties, and consequence of error, under someone else's category scheme. The regulation supplies the categories; the cognitive move is the one the assessment already trained.

Decision Point: Publish the Jailbreak?

Commit Before You Reveal

During an authorized engagement, the red team develops a novel technique that reliably bypasses the guardrail stack the vendor sells to hundreds of other security teams. The vendor's fix will take an estimated six months. A practitioner conference's call for papers closes Friday.

As team lead, choose one of the three options below. Before revealing, settle three things: which option to commit to, which two of the four boundary criteria (authorization, scope, reversibility, accountability) carry the most weight in the argument, and what the strongest objection to that choice would be. "It depends" is not an available conclusion.

The field has not settled this question. Here are possible arguments for each position. Use them to check whether your own reasoning engaged the real trade-offs or argued against a weaker version of the opposing view.

The case for publishing. Hundreds of teams are deploying a control with a known reliable bypass; six months of silence trains no defenders, and capable attackers rediscover techniques independently. Publication with the vendor notified first is how the security field built its entire defensive knowledge base. The accountability criterion: publishing puts the team's name and reasoning on the record. The cost of this choice: some attackers who would not have found the technique now have it, and the vendor's other customers absorb that risk without having chosen it.

The case for vendor-only disclosure. Coordinated disclosure exists because publication timing changes who benefits first, and here there is a single vendor who can ship a fix. The scope criterion: the engagement authorized testing one deployment, not arming the public against everyone's. The cost of this choice: six months is a long exposure window even by the vendor's own estimate, other customers stay untrained and unwarned, and history says vendor timelines slip when the pressure of a disclosure deadline is removed.

The case for holding entirely. A technique that defeats a widely deployed control class is closer to a capability than a bug report; no single door to knock on means no coordinated fix, and reversibility is zero once released. The cost of this choice: defenders stay blind, the technique's rediscovery is a matter of time, and holding concentrates the knowledge in exactly two places (the team and whoever finds it next) with no accountability structure around either.

Now check your own reasoning. Look at the objection you wrote before revealing: did it engage one of the other two positions as described above, or did it argue against a simplified version? If it was the simplified version, go back and try again with the harder form. Reasonable people will land in different places here, and that is the point. Disclosure norms for AI capabilities are still being worked out, and this kind of disagreement is part of the territory.

Knowledge Check

Your team is deploying an LLM-based fraud detector for a bank with EU operations. A colleague asks whether the EU AI Act applies. What is the most likely next step?

1. Map the findings to the draft security profile. NIST has published a draft profile that connects the AI RMF to the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) that most security teams already work with. Take the top three recommendations from the model assessment and find where each one lands in that profile's structure. Then look for one finding that does not fit cleanly into any category. That gap tells us something about the limits of the current taxonomy, similar to what we saw when mapping attacks across taxonomies earlier in the course.

2. Red-team the policy statements. Load a local model, give it NorthShore's acceptable-use statements from Section 3 of this module (The Responsible AI-Use Policy), and prompt it to find loopholes and ambiguities. Then validate each claimed loophole against the actual statement text before accepting it, because the model may confidently describe loopholes in rules that do not actually say what it claims. The real exercise is counting how many of the claimed loopholes hold up when checked against the text.
