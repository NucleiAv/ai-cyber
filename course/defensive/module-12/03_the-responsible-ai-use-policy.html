A Control or a Wish

Two sentences, both representative of real-world policy templates in circulation:

Exhibit A

"Employees are expected to use AI tools ethically and responsibly, in alignment with our organizational values."

Exhibit B

"No data classified CONFIDENTIAL or above may be entered into any AI tool not listed in Appendix A; violations are logged by the and reviewed weekly by the security manager."

One of these functions as a control. The other is a wish. This section covers how to tell the difference and what makes a policy statement actually enforceable.

Here is the engineering reading of a : it is the cheapest control in the stack. It runs before any prompt is sent, costs nothing per invocation, and most often fails when it was written badly or never enforced. Defense-in-depth logic applies directly: policy is the outermost layer, preventing incidents that monitoring would otherwise have to catch.

For a statement to function as a control, it needs four load-bearing parts:

  • Actor (who): the role responsible for the action.
  • Object (what): the data, tool, or output the rule governs.
  • Condition (when, under what circumstances): the trigger or scope that tells someone whether this rule applies right now.
  • Observable evidence (how anyone would know): a log, a checkbox, a countersignature, something an auditor can point to after the fact.

Enforceability adds a fifth:

  • Mechanism + Owner: a technical or procedural enforcement path, and a named role responsible for operating it. Without this, a statement can be testable on paper but toothless in practice.

Go back and re-read the two exhibits with that anatomy in mind. Exhibit A has an actor and nothing else: no object, no condition, no evidence, no enforcement. Exhibit B has all five, which is why an analyst mid-task can consult it and get an answer, and why an auditor after an incident can determine whether it was followed.

The Testability Criterion

A policy statement functions as a control when an analyst can tell whether a specific action violates it. is the main quality bar we apply to statements throughout this section: does the statement name an actor, an object, a condition, and observable evidence? For enforceability, does it also specify a mechanism and an owner?

The Anatomy of a Security Team's AI Policy

Nine sections, each doing distinct work.

#Policy sectionThe work it does
1 Scope Which teams, which tools, which data. A policy that does not name its own boundaries invites the "I didn't think it applied to that" defense.
2 Acceptable use What may be done, by which role, with which approved tools. Approved-tool lists live in an appendix so the policy body can survive tool churn.
3 Prohibited use The bright lines, stated as observable actions, not values.
4 Data handling What may enter which tool, expressed in the organization's existing data classification vocabulary. This is where gets addressed, and the section only works if it is specific enough to enforce.
5 Validation mandates Which output classes require verification, against what, and before what action. The verification habit this course has practiced since the first hands-on week gets written into enforceable language here as a .
6 Human oversight requirements Which decision classes require sign-off, by whom, and recorded where. The oversight-engineering findings from Section 2 translate directly here: decision-class routing, rationale requirements, calibrated volume.
7 AI incident response What counts as an , severity tiers, escalation owner, rollback authority. The AI-specific incident response material we studied for technical containment gets its organizational counterpart here: who declares an incident, who decides to roll back, and who discloses.
8 Review cadence When the policy itself is re-reviewed, and what triggers an off-cycle review: a new tool class, a new regulation, an incident.
9 Accountability Named roles, not named people, who own the policy, the tool list, the exceptions process, and enforcement.

A Validation Mandate That Works

The scenario organization is NorthShore Mutual, a fictional mid-size financial-services SOC. (the full brief arrives in the next section.) NorthShore runs an triage assistant. Here is a validation-mandate section drafted for it, with the anatomy marked in small caps.

5.2 Before an alert classified Critical or High by the triage assistant is escalated to incident response, the assigned analyst [actor] must verify the classification basis [object]: confirm the referenced log entries exist and support the stated severity [condition/procedure]. Verification is recorded as a checkbox-plus-comment in the ticket [evidence]. Escalations lacking a verification record are returned by the IR lead [enforcement + owner].

5.3 Enrichment text generated by the triage assistant (suggested technique mappings, investigation notes) is advisory. It may not be copied into incident reports without analyst confirmation of each factual claim; the confirming analyst's initials appear on the report section [actor, object, condition, evidence].

Why 5.2 works: clause by clause

Each design decision in 5.2 maps back to something we have already covered. Take it one clause at a time:

  • Scoped to Critical/High only. Mandating verification of every alert would recreate the 200-clicks problem and produce rubber-stamp verification of nothing. The scope keeps the volume reviewable.
  • Concrete verification procedure. "Confirm the referenced log entries exist and support the stated severity" gives the analyst a specific check, so "verify" cannot degrade into "glance."
  • Creates evidence. The checkbox-plus-comment in the ticket is an audit trail. Without a record, the mandate is unauditable and effectively optional.
  • Enforcement assigned to a role with a natural incentive. The IR lead returns escalations that lack a verification record. That role already owns the cost of bad escalations, so enforcement aligns with existing accountability rather than adding a new bureaucratic layer.

The Honest Limit of the Instrument

Notice what 5.2 does not do: it does not prevent an analyst from pencil-whipping the checkbox. Policy can make verification checkable and negligence traceable, but it cannot force anyone to actually think. That is why policy is one layer in a defense-in-depth stack, not a substitute for the others.

Is This Statement a Control?

Reasoning Task: Six Candidate Statements

For each statement, decide: testable or aspirational? For the aspirational ones, work out what a testable version would need to add before an analyst could tell whether a specific action violates it. One statement is testable but unenforceable as written; find it and name the missing mechanism.

  1. "Analysts should use AI tools responsibly."
  2. "No case data classified CONFIDENTIAL or above may be entered into any AI tool not on the approved list in Appendix A."
  3. "AI outputs must be validated before action."
  4. "Model-assisted verdicts on Critical alerts require analyst sign-off recorded in the ticket."
  5. "Analysts must remain vigilant about hallucinated content."
  6. "Prompts containing customer (personally identifiable information) are logged by the gateway and flagged for weekly review."

1. Aspirational. No object, condition, or evidence. A rewrite must name tools, data, and actions: "Analysts may use only the tools in Appendix A for case work, and may not enter case data above INTERNAL classification into any of them."

2. Testable, and enforceable if a logging or DLP mechanism exists. As a bare sentence it is testable but unenforced, which is acceptable only if the accountability section assigns the mechanism.

3. The trap. Testable in principle, but unenforceable as written: no defined procedure, no record, no owner. Compare it to worked example 5.2, which is the same idea with its missing parts filled in.

4. Testable and enforceable. Actor, object, condition, evidence, all present; the ticket record is the audit trail.

5. Aspirational. "Vigilance" is a state of mind, not an observable action. A rewrite needs to specify which claims get checked, against what, and recorded where.

6. Testable and enforceable, and worth noticing as an oversight-engineering control: sampling-based review at calibrated volume, exactly the design the automation-bias evidence recommends.
