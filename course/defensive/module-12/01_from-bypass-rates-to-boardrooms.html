The Decision Nobody Assigned

Last week's takeaway was that every defense layer we examined had a documented bypass rate. Input filters catch known patterns but miss encoded ones. models that block 85% of attack prompts in testing drop to a third of that against unseen attacks. Monitoring is there because everything in front of it leaks. reduces risk, but nothing eliminates it.

So the deployment question is never "is it safe?" It is "how much are we accepting, and is that acceptable for this use?" That question has no technical answer. A model cannot accept risk, and a guardrail cannot be accountable. Somewhere, a person has to sign off, and when nobody does, the organization ends up accepting risk it never measured through a decision nobody realized was being made.

is the structure that makes that decision explicit: who has authority to approve a deployment, against what criteria, with what oversight afterward, and who answers when the system fails. Technical controls constrain what a system can do; governance constrains what an organization decides to do with it, and assigns accountability for whatever risk remains.

A Documented Case: Nobody Owned the Chatbot's Answers

Air Canada put a support chatbot on its website. Asked about bereavement fares, it told a customer he could book at full price and apply for the reduced rate within ninety days. That was not the airline's policy. When the customer asked for the difference back and was refused, he took the matter to British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal.

The airline's defense is the part that matters here. Air Canada argued that the correct policy was published elsewhere on its own site, and that the chatbot was, in the tribunal's summary of the submission, "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions." The tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and rejected it: the airline is responsible for everything on its website, and "it makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." It found that Air Canada had not taken reasonable care to ensure the chatbot was accurate.

The money involved was a few hundred dollars. What the case shows is the shape of the failure rather than its size. Nobody at the airline had been assigned the question of whether the chatbot's answers were correct, or who would answer for them when they were not, and the first instinct once it failed was to argue that the tool owned the decision. A tool cannot. That is why the rest of this module is about who does.

Residual Risk Is Accepted by People, Not Tools

When no control is sufficient, deployment is a risk-acceptance decision. Governance is how an organization decides, on purpose, how much risk to accept, and who owns that choice.

Security teams end up owning this work for a practical reason: they are usually the only people in the organization who already run risk-based control programs. The skills transfer directly, and much of what this section covers builds on evaluation and risk concepts we have already practiced.

Four Functions, One Hub

The (published January 2023) organizes AI risk work into four functions. Each one is really a question that an assessor keeps asking for as long as the system runs.

Govern: who is accountable?

Does the organization have policies, roles, decision authority, and a risk culture for AI, and do they actually operate? is not a phase an organization completes and moves past; it is the central hub that connects to every other function and runs for the system's entire life.

Map: what is this system, really?

What is it for, who does it affect, what does it connect to, and what happens when it is wrong? happens before deployment, and then again whenever the context changes.

Measure: how do we know?

Which risks are tracked, with what metrics, how often, and are those metrics even the right ones? The evaluation vocabulary we have used all semester (precision, recall, false positive rates) lives in . The next section pushes it one step further.

Manage: what do we do about it?

How are identified risks prioritized, treated, monitored, and responded to when they materialize? is where risk treatment and incident response live.

The most common mistake is reading these as sequential steps, one through four. An organization does not "finish" Govern in January and move on. If an auditor asks in month eight, "who approved this?", that is a Govern question about a live system, and when the answer is "nobody knows," that is a Govern gap the system has been running with the entire time.

4

The Framework as an Instrument

A framework is useful when we can pick it up and use it as an assessment instrument, not just read it. The process is one we have already practiced with the OWASP checklist against application deployments, and it follows the same four steps: decide what applies, look for evidence of existing controls, grade the gap, recommend a fix. The only thing that changes is the categories. categories name technical failure classes; RMF functions name organizational outcomes.

The beatOWASP defensive assessment asksRMF gap assessment asks
Applicability Does this risk category apply to this deployment pattern? What does this function expect for a system of this consequence?
Evidence What controls exist? (filters, guardrails, rate limits) What structures exist? (policies, owners, review records, metrics)
Grade Covered, partially covered, or exposed Present, partial, or absent
Recommendation A named control ("add output schema validation") A named artifact with an owner ("a pre-deployment review gate owned by security architecture")

The unit of work is the . A finding has three parts:

  1. 1

    Expected Outcome

    What should be in place. For example: "Someone with named authority approves AI deployments against written criteria."

  2. 2

    Evidence Found or Not Found

    What actually exists. For example: "Procurement records show the triage assistant was purchased and deployed by the lead; no approval criteria exist in any policy document."

  3. 3

    Grade

    Present, partial, or absent.

A finding without evidence is just an opinion. Assessment reports need to be grounded in what actually exists (or does not) on the ground.

Misconception: The RMF Is a Compliance Checklist

The AI RMF is voluntary. No regulator grades it, and it prescribes no specific controls. That cuts both ways. On the upside, it structures judgment rather than replacing it: the output of an assessment is findings and argued recommendations, not a pass/fail stamp. On the downside, an organization can wave at the framework, produce a binder, and change nothing. A policy PDF nobody follows is still a Govern gap, regardless of how polished the document looks.

There is a live professional debate about whether a framework built for AI systems in general is specific enough for security operations. A draft companion profile currently in progress tries to bridge the AI RMF to the cybersecurity framework that security teams already use, which is evidence both that the need is real and that the base framework leaves gaps. Practitioners on one side argue the RMF's generality is what lets it cover fast-moving technology; those on the other argue that voluntary, non-prescriptive guidance produces binders, not behavior. This course does not take a side. We use the framework as an assessment instrument and acknowledge its limits.

Worked Example: Mapping the Triage Pipeline

Let's run one function, , against a deployment we know from the inside: the LLM-assisted alert triage pipeline we built earlier in the course (parse, classify severity, enrich, hand off). Suppose a mid-size has been running it in production for six months.

Read Before Continuing: The RMF Core

Before working through this example, read Part 2 (the Core) of the NIST AI RMF. Read for the shape of each function, holding one question open: "for this triage pipeline, what evidence would show this function is present?" Skip the appendices. About 30 minutes.

  1. 1

    Pick the function's guiding questions

    For Map, two will do the work here: "What decisions does this system's output feed, and what is the consequence of a wrong one?" and "Who is affected by its errors, and do they know it is in use?"

  2. 2

    Hunt for evidence

    The pipeline's output feeds analyst attention: a Critical alert gets investigated within the hour while a Low sits in the queue. A misclassification therefore delays incident response, which has a measurable cost. The affected parties are analysts (whose workload the system shapes) and every user whose incident gets deprioritized. Is any of that written down? In most deployments we would find a purchase justification and nothing else. No documented failure-consequence analysis, and no statement of who reviews severity distributions for drift.

  3. 3

    Grade it

    Partial. People can explain informally what the system is for, but the consequence analysis and affected-party mapping exist in nobody's head and no document. Informal understanding does not survive staff turnover, and it cannot be audited.

  4. 4

    Recommend

    Name the , not the . Not "improve documentation" but "a one-page system context record: purpose, decision consumers, failure consequences by severity class, affected parties, review owner, re-reviewed on any workflow change."

Notice what the assessment did not require: any new technical knowledge. Every fact we used came from understanding the deployment, and that pattern holds for the rest of the module. The governance layer uses knowledge we already have; it just organizes it around questions we have not been asking.

Notice where Step 2's missing evidence would have to come from: policies, review records, named owners. Those are all things Govern is responsible for creating. When a Map assessment keeps finding "no document exists, no owner exists," the gaps trace back to the organization never setting up the accountability structures that Govern requires. That is why the four functions interlock rather than running in sequence.

Sort the Failure

Reasoning Task: Three Failures, Four Functions

Three failures, all from deployments this course has examined. For each one, decide which RMF function the failure lives in and what evidence of that function's presence would have looked like.

  • (a) An autonomous agent with write access deletes a production database. Afterward, it turns out that no person or body ever had the authority to approve or reject the agent's permissions before it went live.
  • (b) An alert triage pipeline has been running for a year. Nobody has ever checked whether its severity classifications are equally reliable across the organization's business units.
  • (c) A customer-facing chatbot logs prompt-injection attempts faithfully, but no procedure exists for what to do when one is found. The logs just accumulate, unread.

Then the harder question: in failure (a), the damage happened at run time, during operation. So why is it still not a Manage failure?

(a) Govern. Evidence would be an approval record: a named authority, written criteria, a decision. The run-time damage is downstream of a decision structure that never existed. Manage covers how identified risks are handled, but nothing here was ever identified because nobody was assigned to ask.

(b) Measure. Evidence: a recurring disaggregated evaluation report, reviewed by a named owner. (The next section makes this one concrete, with arithmetic.)

(c) Manage. Evidence would be a response procedure with thresholds, owners, and escalation. Detection without response is a Manage gap even when the logging itself works perfectly.

The useful takeaway is that where a failure shows up is not necessarily where the gap is. Damage shows up operationally, but the gap that permitted it often sits in Govern.
