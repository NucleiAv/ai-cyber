Bias: The Same Confusion Matrix, Sliced

Bias in a detection system means different populations get different error rates. Those differences can enter through four routes, and none of them require anyone to intend anything.

DoorHow bias entersWhat it looks like in practice
Training data One population is underrepresented, so the model generalizes worse for it A detector trained mostly on headquarters traffic misreads subsidiary traffic patterns
Labels The humans who labeled the training data made systematically different judgments about one group's data Annotators rate one group's writing style as more suspicious, and the model learns their judgment faithfully
Proxies A feature correlated with group membership lets the model use group membership without being given it Writing style, network origin, or business hours stand in for who the user is
Thresholds A single operating point tuned on aggregate data sits differently for different populations One threshold is comfortable for the majority group and terrible for a minority group

In AI auditing and ML fairness work, means systematically different error rates for different populations under the same threshold. Intent is not part of the definition. That matters operationally, because it means the right investigation is not about finding someone to blame; it is about computing the per group instead of once. That per-group breakdown is called .

Two Documented Cases

1

Face recognition: which error rate we measure decides who is harmed

The US national standards body evaluated 189 commercial face recognition algorithms and published demographic breakdowns. False positive rates () varied across demographic groups by factors that reached into the tens and hundreds for some algorithms, while false negative variation stayed comparatively small.

That asymmetry matters. A in face identification is a missed match: an inconvenience. A means the system declares this person is that person, and in a security or policing context that can mean a false accusation. Which error type we measure determines which harm we see and which group bears it. An evaluation reporting only aggregate accuracy would have caught none of this.

2

Abuse detection: the labelers taught the model their judgment

Researchers studying toxicity classifiers (the models that flag abusive text for content moderation) found that false positive rates were systematically higher for text written in what the reviewing linguists classified as African American Vernacular English (AAVE). When they dug into why, the cause traced back to annotation: human labelers had rated dialect features themselves as markers of toxicity, and the models faithfully learned that judgment.

This is the labels route in action: a detector doing security-adjacent work, carrying an FPR that differs by group, and nobody would have noticed unless someone computed a per-group confusion matrix.

Guided Analysis: Both Cases, Two Questions

Before revealing: (1) In the face recognition evaluations, which error type drove the disparity, and why does the false-positive/false-negative distinction change who is harmed? (2) In both cases, which function does a "demographic breakdown required" rule belong to, and what recurring artifact would implement it?

(1) False positives drove the face recognition disparity, varying by orders of magnitude while false negatives varied modestly. The distinction matters because the two errors harm differently: a false negative inconveniences the person being matched; a false positive asserts an identity that is not theirs, and the downstream consequence (a stop, an accusation, an account action) lands on someone who did nothing. A system with "good accuracy" can concentrate its worst error type on one group.

(2) Measure. The metric choice is itself a risk decision, and the artifact is a recurring disaggregated evaluation report with a named review owner. In the abuse-detection case, the same artifact would have surfaced the dialect disparity the first time it ran; the fix then targets the mechanism (annotation guidelines and labeler calibration), not the headline number.

Check 4: The Adversary's Read

Suppose an attacker learns the per-population rates we just computed. Where do they route their phishing campaign, and why does that make this a security finding and not only a fairness finding? Settle both answers before revealing.

The routing. Through Population B: impersonate subsidiary staff, target subsidiary inboxes, write in the register the model handles worst. At 0.80 recall, one in five phishing attempts against Population B already gets through, and the 6.0% false positive rate means the subsidiary's own users have learned to distrust the flags they do see.

Why it is a security finding. The disparity is simultaneously a harm distribution and an attack surface: the population the model serves worst is the population an informed attacker will impersonate, target, or route through. The fairness gap and the detection gap are the same number, read two ways. We covered evasion as an attack class earlier in the course; here it intersects with demographics.

Full computed values, for checking your arithmetic. Aggregate: precision 528/572 ≈ 0.92, recall 528/600 = 0.88, FPR 44/1,400 ≈ 3.1%. Population A: precision 368/388 ≈ 0.95, recall 0.92, FPR 2.0%. Population B: precision 160/184 ≈ 0.87, recall 0.80, FPR 6.0%.

One more thing before leaving the lab: neither population is defined by a protected attribute. Business unit and language patterns are , and that is exactly how bias shows up in systems that never see a group label.

Suppose the lab's disparity is confirmed and we set out to fix it. Fix it to what? There are three fairness properties, and each defines "fair" differently:

  • : equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates across groups. The strictest of the three.
  • : equal detection rates across groups, without constraining false positive rates. A relaxation of equalized odds.
  • : the classifier flags the same proportion of each group's cases, regardless of whether the groups actually have different base rates.

Any deployment has to pick which of these three properties it commits to and justify why. The full mathematics belong to a dedicated fairness & bias course. What matters here is the professional discipline: choose explicitly, document the reasoning, and revisit it. A team that never made an explicit choice still ended up with one; they just cannot explain or defend it.

Surveillance: Proportionality Has Case Law

Security work involves surveillance. Logs, monitoring, behavioral analytics: the defensive toolkit watches people. AI multiplies what that watching can extract, and the legal limits have started catching up. Two court decisions show where the lines are forming.

The scraped face database

Source: The Secretive Company That Might End Privacy as We Know It, The New York Times (2020).

ClearView AI built a face identification service for police clients by scraping billions of photos from public websites. The company argued that publicly available data was fair game. European data protection regulators across at least four countries disagreed, issuing fines totaling tens of millions of euros. A UK tribunal confirmed in late 2025 that the company falls under UK data protection law even though it has no UK operations and serves only foreign government clients.

The core principle: "publicly available" does not mean "freely usable." Collecting data at scale for a new purpose (biometric identification) is a new processing activity that needs its own justification. That is , and it reaches directly into work this course has already practiced. Threat intelligence routinely relies on scraped, public data. The lawful question is not "could we access it?" but "what purpose did we collect it for, and is that purpose justified?"

The welfare-fraud scorer

In 2020, a Dutch court struck down a government system that scored neighborhoods for welfare-fraud risk, deployed overwhelmingly in low-income districts. The court did not say fraud detection is illegitimate. It applied a test: the intrusion (broad data linkage across government databases, secret risk models, scoring of whole neighborhoods) was not proportional to the stated aim. The system's opacity made it worse. The consequences went beyond the ruling: thousands of families had been wrongly accused of child welfare fraud and ordered to repay benefits, and the resulting scandal forced the Dutch government to resign in January 2021.

Proportionality is essentially a question with legal force behind it: what exactly does this system do to the people in its scope, and does the security aim justify that specific intrusion? A team that can answer that in writing before deployment is doing governance. A team that cannot will eventually have a court answer it for them.

Resolving the Monitoring-Privacy Tension

The tension we left unresolved last week (abuse monitoring versus analyst and user privacy) resolves the way these cases suggest: not by choosing monitoring or privacy, but by making documented decisions. Minimize what the abuse-monitoring pipeline collects. Set retention limits. Write down the proportionality reasoning while the deployment is still on paper. The tension does not go away, but it turns into something we manage with explicit rules rather than something we ignore.

Guided Analysis: The Two Rulings

Before revealing: (1) State the welfare-fraud court's proportionality test in your own words. (2) List two Map questions that, asked honestly before deployment, would have surfaced the problem. (3) What did the "public data is free data" defense miss, and what does that imply for threat intelligence built on open, publicly available sources?

(1) A workable restatement: the more intrusive the system, the stronger and more specific the justification must be, and the intrusion must be scaled to the aim rather than applied broadly because it is convenient. Secret models and neighborhood-wide scoring failed both halves.

(2) "Who is affected by this system's errors, and are they concentrated in one population?" and "What exactly does the system collect and link, and is each element necessary for the stated aim?" Either question, answered in writing, exposes the design before a court has to.

(3) The defense missed purpose limitation: access is not authorization. Collection at scale for a new purpose is a new processing activity. For threat intelligence, the working rule is to document what is collected, for what purpose, and why that purpose justifies it, because "it was public" has now lost in multiple jurisdictions.

Dual-Use: Governing the Use, Not the Tool

has come up repeatedly throughout the course: the same model that triages alerts can write phishing lures; the same skills that find vulnerabilities can enable exploits. The governance takeaway is straightforward: the technology is not going to sort itself into defensive and offensive categories, so governance has to regulate use and context instead. The four criteria we developed for offensive work (authorization, scope, reversibility, accountability) are already , and they slot directly into the Govern function as policy content. The next section writes them into policy language.

One dual-use problem remains genuinely unsettled, and this module will not pretend otherwise: disclosure of AI attack techniques. Traditional vulnerability disclosure works because there is a single vendor who can identify the flaw in a codebase and ship a patch. AI jailbreaks do not fit that model cleanly, even for a single vendor.

As we saw in the defending-AI-systems module, patching a jailbreak is not like patching a buffer overflow: guardrail fixes are probabilistic, retraining can shift behavior elsewhere, and the guardrail-robustness evaluations we studied showed bypass rates climbing back up against unseen prompts. A technique that works across many models from different vendors compounds the problem further, since there is no coordinated fix path.

Meanwhile, holding the technique private leaves defenders untrained while capable attackers rediscover it independently. The security community has three decades of practice with this tradeoff for software flaws but no settled equivalent for AI capabilities. The discussion at the end of this module asks for a committed position, and there are defensible arguments on multiple sides.

Automated Decisions: The Loop Is Not the Fix

The last concern is automating consequential security decisions: account lockout, access revocation, alert dismissal, attribution. The usual safeguard is "keep a human in the loop," which here means the runtime sense of the phrase distinguished in the first module: a named person approving a specific action, not the raters aggregated into the model during training. The evidence shows that having a human in the loop does not automatically produce meaningful oversight.

A nine-country experiment with roughly 9,000 participants looked at how people weigh AI recommendations in high-stakes decisions. It found systematic over-trust that varied with the participant's AI knowledge: people with little exposure over-trusted the most, but even experienced participants showed calibration failures. That documented tendency has a name: .

Illustration: cloud-science.de

Think about a where an analyst faces 200 model-recommended lockouts per shift, each one an approve-button click away. The analyst approves 99% within seconds. On paper, a human reviewed every decision. In practice, nobody reviewed anything; the volume and the interface made rubber-stamping the path of least resistance. The automation-bias evidence explains why this happens so reliably.

For oversight to actually work, it needs to be engineered like any other control, with its own failure modes accounted for:

  • Route only consequential decision classes to humans, at a volume a person can actually scrutinize.
  • Force engagement: a required one-line rationale changes reading behavior in a way a click cannot.
  • Preserve the ability to dissent, including the workflow time to exercise it.
  • Audit the oversight itself: an approval rate near 100% is a signal the control has failed, not that the model is excellent.

Regulation is arriving at the same conclusion from the legal side. For high-risk systems, the EU's AI rules require that human overseers be able to understand, monitor, and override the system, and that they be aware of the tendency to over-rely. The reading assigned later in this module includes that article. When reading it, notice how much engineering work the legal requirements actually imply.

This ties back to the accountability question from earlier: who owns the decision when AI-assisted triage goes wrong? The governance answer is that ownership gets assigned before deployment, per decision class, in writing. The next section turns that into policy language.
