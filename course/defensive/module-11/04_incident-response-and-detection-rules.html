AI-Specific Incident Response

The process is familiar: detect, contain, eradicate, recover, learn. The framework provides the structure, and most organizations have adapted it to their needs. AI systems do not invalidate that framework, but they break specific assumptions that traditional incident response takes for granted.

Traditional incident response assumes deterministic systems with static evidence. A compromised server produces forensic images, log files, network captures. The evidence generally does not change after collection. Running the same malware in a sandbox tends to produce the same behavior. The incident is typically contained once the compromised system is isolated.

AI systems violate each of those assumptions. The names three ways they break:

  1. 1

    Probabilistic evidence

    The same input to an LLM may produce a different output on replay. Reproducing an incident for forensic analysis may not be possible. An attacker's successful prompt injection might fail on replay because the model's response depends on the full , including previous conversation turns that may not have been logged.

  2. 2

    Ephemeral state

    Context windows, session state, and in-memory caches may not be preserved after the session ends. Unlike a hard drive image, the model's internal state during the incident may be unrecoverable.

  3. 3

    Continuous harm

    A poisoned knowledge base can continue serving adversarial content to users until the poisoned documents are identified and removed. A compromised model may not stop producing harmful output when the team "isolates" it from the network. Containment requires remediating the model or data source itself, not just the network path.

These differences change how an incident response team operates at each phase.

Logging: Capture full prompts and responses, not just metadata. In traditional incident response, you can often reconstruct an attack from network logs and file system changes. In AI incident response, the model's behavior during the incident may be irreproducible, so the primary evidence is what you logged at the time. "User sent a message, model responded" gives you little to investigate. Full prompts, full responses, guardrail verdicts, retrieval results, and session context let you reconstruct what happened and why.

Containment: Network isolation is necessary but not sufficient. Containment must include model-level actions: disable the endpoint, revert to a known-good checkpoint, quarantine the knowledge base if retrieval content is suspected. A poisoned RAG knowledge base can keep serving adversarial content even after you cut the network cable, because the poisoned documents are inside the system's own data store.

Recovery: Verify that the root cause is remediated in the model or data, not just in the infrastructure. If the incident was , recovery means identifying and removing every poisoned document, verifying the model's behavior returns to baseline, and monitoring for re-injection. If the incident was a compromised model, recovery means replacing it with a known-good version and auditing how the compromise occurred.

Denial-of-wallet as a monitoring problem

attacks exploit pay-per-use pricing to generate massive bills. Documented incidents have produced compute costs in the tens of thousands of dollars per day from a single compromised API credential.

This is a monitoring problem because the technical behavior (API calls, model responses) may look entirely normal. The attacker is using the API exactly as intended; they are just not authorized to do so, or they are consuming resources at a financially destructive rate. Request-count rate limiting is insufficient because one long-context request can cost as much as dozens of small ones. , per-user spending caps, and billing anomaly alerts are the relevant controls.

The monitoring-privacy tension

Effective abuse monitoring requires logging user interactions: prompts, responses, session metadata. recommends prompt log retention for security monitoring. Privacy regulations may constrain how long logs can be retained, who can access them, and whether users must be informed. Those two requirements pull in opposite directions.

This tension is unresolved in the field. Different organizations, jurisdictions, and deployment contexts will draw the line between security monitoring and user privacy in different places. What matters here is recognizing that the tension exists and being able to articulate both sides when making or evaluating monitoring design decisions.

Detection Rule Design

Scenario: Financial Services Chatbot Anomaly

A monitoring dashboard for a financial services chatbot shows the following anomaly from a single user session:

  • 847 queries in one day (system average: 12 per user per day)
  • 38% of queries flagged by the input guardrail
  • Average response length: 1,842 tokens (system average: 340 tokens)
  • 14 queries contained Base64-encoded segments
  • Session topic distribution: 72% "internal procedures," 18% "system configuration," 10% "product information"

Design a compound detection rule. For each element, specify: (a) the detection type, (b) the specific indicator and threshold, (c) the alert action, and (d) one false-positive scenario where a legitimate user might trigger this indicator.

A well-designed compound rule might combine three signals:

First, rate-based: daily query count exceeding 100 (847 is 70x the average). Catches volume-based attacks. False-positive: a power user conducting extensive product research before a major purchase.

Second, content-based: more than 5 Base64-encoded segments per session. Catches encoding-based evasion attempts. False-positive: a developer asking the chatbot technical questions about Base64 encoding itself.

Third, behavioral: average response length exceeding 3x the system average (1,020+ tokens when the average is 340). Catches successful data extraction where the model returns more content than intended. False-positive: a user asking complex multi-part questions that legitimately require detailed responses.

The compound rule triggers an alert only when two or more of these three signals fire simultaneously. The 38% guardrail flag rate is informative but should not be part of the compound rule itself because it indicates the guardrails are working. The 62% of queries that passed guardrails while the user was clearly probing is more concerning.

Alert action: flag the session for human review, temporarily reduce the user's rate limit, and preserve the full prompt/response log for investigation.

Design a Compound Rule for Session E

Session E triggered signals across all three detection layers. Design a compound detection rule that would catch this session while minimizing . Specify: (a) which signals to combine, (b) the threshold for each, (c) how many must fire before alerting, and (d) what the alert action should be.

A well-designed compound rule for this scenario combines three signals with a two-of-three trigger:

Rate-based: daily query count exceeding 100 (Session E's 847 is 70x the system average of 12). Threshold set high enough that power users like Session F (22 queries) do not trigger it.

Content-based: more than 5 Base64-encoded segments per session. Session E had 14. Legitimate use of Base64 in a financial services chatbot is rare; a threshold of 5 allows some margin for edge cases like developers pasting encoded data.

Behavioral: average response length exceeding 3x the system average (1,020+ tokens when the average is 340). Session E's 1,842-token average clears this threshold. High response length combined with anomalous topic distribution (72% internal procedures) strongly suggests data extraction.

Trigger: alert when two or more of these three signals fire simultaneously. Session E fires all three. The 38% guardrail flag rate is informative context but should not be part of the compound rule because it indicates controls are partially working; the 62% that got through is the concern.

Alert action: flag the session for immediate human review, temporarily reduce the user's rate limit to 10 queries per hour, and preserve the full prompt/response log for forensic investigation.
