Academic Papers & Preprints

  • Hazell, J. (2023). "Spear Phishing with Large Language Models." Preprint
    Controlled study of automated profiling and lure generation. Reports that automated profiling produces accurate target descriptions in a large majority of cases. Source for Section 2's phishing pipeline and effectiveness discussion. Not peer-reviewed.
    arXiv:2305.06972
  • Lermen, S., et al. (2026). "A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using LLMs." USENIX Security 2026. Paper
    Peer-reviewed large-scale effectiveness and scale evidence for AI-generated phishing. Source for Section 2. Read the methodology to understand population and limits.
  • Happe, A., et al. (2025). "On the Surprising Efficacy of LLMs for Penetration-Testing." Preprint
    Capability and limits of AI pen-test agents on controlled targets. Source for Section 3. Not peer-reviewed.
    arXiv:2507.00829
  • RAND Corporation. (2024/2025). "Investigating the Potential Use of Frontier AI Models for Offensive Cyberattacks: A Human Uplift Study." Report
    Commissioned by a national AI security institute. Found that frontier models gave lower-skilled actors only bounded uplift and could not autonomously chain a full campaign. Independent (not an AI vendor), but a controlled experiment whose conditions may not capture every real-world dynamic. Source for Section 4.
    rand.org

Source Caveats

Reading Sources Critically

Every source has a context. When you read security research, keep these caveats in mind:

Source CategoryKey Caveat
AI vendor threat reports (GTIG, Anthropic GTG-1002, OpenAI) Produced by companies with commercial interests that can pull in two directions: sometimes downplaying offensive capability to reassure customers, sometimes emphasizing it to demonstrate the value of their safety work. Treat magnitudes as reported, not settled.
Security vendor analysis (Trend Micro, Unit 42, ENISA headline figures) Vendor methodologies may favor larger numbers or narrower populations. Treat vendor-sourced figures as directional, not authoritative. Cross-check against independent evidence where possible.
Academic preprints (Hazell 2023, Happe et al. 2025) Not peer-reviewed. Cite findings as study-specific on a specific date, not as universal constants. Methodology sections are required reading.
Benchmark results (pen-test agents, deepfake detectors, capture-the-flag studies) Always specify: which dataset, which model, what conditions. Benchmark performance does not equal production performance. Results can be inflated by training-data contamination.
Incident reports (AI Incident Database #634, #1263) Compiled from contemporaneous reporting; details may evolve as investigations proceed. Verify against primary sources before citing in professional work.

A note on statistics in this module

Several widely circulated figures (for example, that AI-supported phishing represents a large majority of social-engineering activity, or large year-over-year percentage increases in vishing and deepfake incidents) originate in vendor or practitioner reporting. Treat such numbers as reported by a specific source, on a specific date, and verify them against a primary source before relying on them. Dating and sourcing every capability and threat claim is itself a skill this module teaches.

Living content (specific cases, tool examples, framework versions, and statistics) should be verified before each term. The full module source bibliography, including source tiers and verification flags, accompanies this module for instructors.
