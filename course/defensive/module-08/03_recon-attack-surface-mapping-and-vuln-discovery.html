Driving Question

When an attacker feeds scan data or code to an AI, how good is the output, and how do we tell its real findings from its fabrications?

1

From Scan Output to AI Summary: Trust and Verification

Social engineering is where AI moved the skill and scale levers furthest. Here the same levers shift for a different phase of an attack: reconnaissance and vulnerability discovery. The "trust but verify" discipline we have practiced all semester applies in a new direction. Instead of evaluating a model's defensive output (an advisory summary, a code review), we evaluate the kind of output an attacker would see when feeding scan data or code into a model.

Key terms

  • : the points where an attacker could enter or extract data.
  • : information-gathering before any system is acted upon.
  • : a proposed chain of weaknesses leading to a higher-impact outcome.
  • : feeding varied or malformed inputs to a program to provoke revealing crashes.
  • : plausible-sounding but false output; here, an unsupported exploit step.

The basic mechanism

A network scan produces structured facts: which hosts are up, which ports are open, which service versions are reported, which known vulnerabilities a scanner flagged. A language model can ingest that output and produce two useful-looking things: a readable summary of the attack surface and a proposed, prioritized exploit path.

The appeal is obvious. A single scan can be thousands of lines, and a model can compress it into a paragraph and a plan in seconds. The same technique works on the defender's side: pipe your own scan into a model and get a human-readable summary of what is exposed. The lever that moves is time, specifically the time from raw output to actionable summary.

Two Failure Modes: Context Overflow and Hallucinated Exploits

Context-Window Overflow

A long scan can exceed the model's . Once later content pushes earlier findings out of the window, the model effectively forgets hosts it already saw, and its summary silently omits them. The summary reads as complete but is not. There is no error message; the omission is invisible unless you compare the summary against the original scan line by line.

Hallucinated Exploit Paths

The model does not verify anything; it predicts plausible text. So it will sometimes invent a service version that was not in the scan, or propose an exploit step that sounds right for the kind of system described but is not supported by the actual findings. That is in an operational setting, and it is dangerous precisely because a fabricated exploit path reads exactly like a real one.

Trustworthy vs. fabricated: a side-by-side comparison

Suppose the simulated scan reports a single fact for one host: TCP port 22 is open.

ElementTrustworthy SummaryHallucinated Summary
Claim "Host A exposes SSH on port 22; service version not identified." "Host A runs OpenSSH 9.6, which is vulnerable to a known remote code execution flaw; an exploit is available."
Source line in scan Yes: the scan shows port 22 open. No: the scan never reported a version. The version, the vulnerability, and the exploit are all invented.
Confidence tone Cautious. States what is known and what is not. More specific, more useful, more confident, and entirely fabricated.
Verification move None needed for the stated claim. Point to the scan line. "Port 22 open" has one; "OpenSSH 9.6" does not.

The move that catches this is mechanical. For every claim in the summary, point to the line in the scan that supports it. A claim with no supporting line is either plausible-but-unverifiable (we would need to gather more data before acting) or fabricated (it invents beyond or contradicts the data). Common misconceptions

  1. "If the AI lists an exploit path, those steps are real." They may be supported, plausible-but-unverifiable, or fabricated. Each step must be checked against the source data.
  2. "A clean AI summary of a scan means the scan had nothing else." Context-window limits can cause silent omissions. Absence in the summary is not evidence of absence in the scan.

Why this matters operationally

The same technique a defender might use to accelerate an authorized assessment is available to adversaries, so the attack surface an organization leaves exposed gets summarized faster than before. More immediately for our own practice: never act on an AI-proposed exploit path, finding, or remediation without checking it against ground truth. A tester who runs an exploit because the model suggested it, without verifying that the step is real, can waste hours or cause damage chasing a fabrication.

Google's Big Sleep and the Real Discovery Frontier

The previous subsection focused on where models fail when reading scan data. The clearest documented example of where they succeed is Google's Big Sleep project.

Background

Big Sleep grew out of , a collaboration between and . The idea is to pair a language model with a fuzzing-and-debugging loop: the agent reads code, writes and runs its own test inputs in a sandbox, observes the results, and reasons about what to try next. In late 2024, Big Sleep identified a real vulnerability in the widely used , and later a vulnerability tracked as CVE-2025-6965, that traditional fuzzing had missed even after extensive automated testing and many CPU-hours of effort.

What is new, and what is bounded

What Big Sleep DemonstratedWhat It Did Not Demonstrate
An agent combined code reading, input generation, execution, and reasoning in a loop. It did not work autonomously in the wild. It operated inside a purpose-built harness on a specific target.
It found a real bug that a mature, widely used fuzzer had missed. It is not a general "point it at anything and get zero-days" button. The Google team explicitly frames the work as bounded.
The discovery is a genuine capability, not a press release. The vulnerability was assigned a CVE. Generalizing from one scaffolded success to universal autonomous discovery is the .

What it means for defenders

1

Defensive Opportunity

AI-assisted discovery can help us find our own bugs first: the dual-use inversion working in our favor. As the cost of finding certain classes of bugs falls, the value of the secure-by-design practices we studied when reviewing code and supply chains goes up.

2

Strategic Pressure

The attack surface an organization exposes becomes discoverable faster. The response is not to panic but to tighten the surface: fix the bugs that exist before an attacker's agent finds them.

Tradeoffs and open questions

The core tension is speed versus trust. Models compress huge scan outputs quickly, but the compression is lossy and unverified, and the failures are silent. The open question for the field is how much of vulnerability discovery will become reliably automatable. Current evidence shows real but narrow successes, and benchmark results are easy to overstate when a model may have seen the test data during training (the data contamination problem we studied earlier in the course). For now, AI output is a lead to verify, never a finding to act on.

Misconception

"AI finds zero-days on demand now." Documented successes are narrow and scaffolded. Treat the capability as real, bounded, and dated. The honest statement always includes the conditions under which the result was obtained.

Common errors to watch for

"Summarizes scanner output" belongs under Exploitation. It does not. Summarizing is information-gathering, which is Reconnaissance; exploitation begins only when a weakness is actually acted upon.

"Drafting and sending the lure are both Delivery." They are not. Generating the lure is Weaponization; transmitting it is Delivery. Keep the two distinct.
