1

Tracing One Alert End to End: C2 Beaconing

Time to follow the traffic from part 2's third table row, the one the baseline missed, through the whole pipeline. We will walk six steps: the original input, the task, the expected output, a correct interpretation, a realistic failure, and the validation that separates the two.

1

Original input: plain-text resolver and proxy logs

Two log sources, both plain text, one event per line. First, our DNS resolver's query log:

2026-06-08T01:41:02Z client 10.20.31.17 query api-sync-cdn[.]net A
2026-06-08T01:41:02Z client 10.20.31.17 reply api-sync-cdn[.]net A 198.51.100.23

Then the web proxy's connection log for the same host over the following half hour:

2026-06-08T01:41:09Z 10.20.31.17 -> 198.51.100.23:443 TLS bytes_out=51208 bytes_in=4096
2026-06-08T01:47:31Z 10.20.31.17 -> 198.51.100.23:443 TLS bytes_out=18450 bytes_in=2048
2026-06-08T01:53:55Z 10.20.31.17 -> 198.51.100.23:443 TLS bytes_out=12990 bytes_in=2048
2026-06-08T02:00:14Z 10.20.31.17 -> 198.51.100.23:443 TLS bytes_out=17221 bytes_in=2048

A parser turns each line into fields (timestamp, client, destination, byte counts) before anything downstream sees it. The unsupervised detector from part 4 flags the pattern: regular intervals of 5 to 7 minutes with consistent sizes do not look like human browsing. It writes an alert record in the part-3 format with "event_type": "anomalous-periodic-traffic" and a detector_score of 0.91.

2

Task: enrich the flagged alert

The LLM enrichment stage receives the alert record plus the raw lines above. Its task, fixed by our prompt: summarize the evidence, propose a severity from our house scale, recommend a next step, and cite which input lines support each claim. It must not invent facts that are not in the input.

3

Expected output format: the alert record, enriched

The output is not free text. It is the same alert record with the enrichment fields filled in, so it must pass schema validation like everything else:

"severity": "<one of: low | medium | high | critical>",
"rationale": "<plain-language summary citing evidence_refs>",
"recommended_action": "<one of our documented next steps>"
4

A correct interpretation

What a good enrichment looks like

Severity: high. "Host 10.20.31.17 contacted a domain not previously seen in our logs, then began TLS connections to the resolved address at regular 5–7 minute intervals with small, consistent payloads (evidence lines 3–6). The pattern is consistent with C2 beaconing. Domain registration age is unknown from this input. Recommended action: threat-intelligence lookup on the domain and host triage. Not recommended without confirmation: isolation."

Note what makes this good: every claim cites input lines, the unknown (registration age) is declared unknown, and the recommendation is a check, not an irreversible action.

5

A realistic failure: fluent, plausible, and wrong

The same input can produce this

Severity: low. "Periodic small transfers to api-sync-cdn[.]net are characteristic of a software update heartbeat. This domain belongs to a content delivery network registered in 2019 and is a known update endpoint. Recommended action: none."

Every sentence reads confidently. The registration year is fabricated: nothing in the input says when the domain was registered, and the model's knowledge cutoff means it cannot know this domain at all. This is the same failure family we catalogued in Section 2, arriving in production dress.

Side by side, steps 4 and 5 differ in exactly one discipline: the correct output treats missing evidence as missing, while the failure fills the gap with plausible invention. Fluency is identical in both. That is why fluency is not a validation signal.

Validation against ground truth

is not an AI-specific idea. It is the same evaluation concept we used for labeled test sets in Section 2: an independent, authoritative source that settles a claim, whether the claim came from a rule, a model, an LLM, or a person. For this alert, the checks are concrete:

Check Settles which claim Automatic or human
Schema validation of the enriched record Output shape: severity in the allowed set, required fields present Automatic

lookup Whether the domain or IP is already reported by others Automatic

The automatic checks are : an enrichment that fails them never reaches the analyst as a trusted draft. The human checks are why the analyst, not the pipeline, owns the verdict.

The Pattern to Reuse

Input format, task, output contract, interpretation, failure mode, validation. We will apply this same six-step trace to threat intelligence in Module 5. Whenever a tool's output sounds right, the question is never how it sounds. It is which gate would catch it if it were wrong.

ROC Curves and the False-Positive Budget

Pre-reading reminder: this part assumes the Google ML Crash Course material on ROC and AUC (reference page, R2).

The trace in part 6 dealt with one alert. Choosing detectors is a question about all of them at once. Section 2 gave us precision, recall, and the , all measured at a single threshold. But a classifier has a continuum of thresholds, and each one trades true positives against false positives differently. The plots that whole tradeoff space, and summarizes it in one number.

One number is not enough, though, because we will deploy at exactly one . Which point? Our part-1 numbers decide. The arithmetic takes four lines:

The False-Positive Budget, Step by Step

  1. Capacity: our team investigates at most 15 alerts per day.
  2. Expected true positives: about 3 genuine attacks per day, which we want investigated. That uses 3 of the 15 slots.
  3. False-positive budget: 15 − 3 = 12 false alarms per day, at most.
  4. Required FPR: false positive rate is false alarms divided by benign events: 12 / 9,997 ≈ 0.0012. (The denominator is the 10,000 events minus the 3 attacks, not the full 10,000.)

Now suppose we evaluated three candidate systems on the same labeled evaluation set, our ground truth for this comparison. The AUC column comes from each system's full curve; the two TPR columns are read directly off the measured curves at the two operating points we care about. These are measurements from our evaluation, not properties of the algorithms. Another Random Forest on other data would measure differently. This evaluation set was also built with a random split, so the same attack families appear in both the training and test data. That is the setting in which supervised models are expected to do best (Section 3), and so the table describes how each system handles attacks resembling those it was trained on. It says little about novel attacks, which are the main reason to run an unsupervised detector.

System Algorithm AUC Measured TPR at FPR 0.0012 Measured TPR at FPR 0.01
A (supervised) 0.97 0.85 0.98
B (unsupervised) 0.82 0.45 0.74
C (supervised) 0.88 0.60 0.83

Scenario 1: Our Small SOC, FPR Budget 0.0012

Scenario: Picking a System for Our Part-1 SOC

Using the measured table and the FPR budget of 0.0012: how many of our 3 daily attacks does each system catch, on average? Which system would you deploy, and what does the AUC column hide?

Expected catches per day at FPR 0.0012: System A: 0.85 × 3 ≈ 2.6 attacks. System C: 0.60 × 3 ≈ 1.8. System B: 0.45 × 3 ≈ 1.4, meaning B misses more attacks than it catches at this operating point.

The decision: System A is the only candidate that catches most of the attacks in this evaluation inside our budget. The AUC gap between A and C looks modest (0.97 vs. 0.88), but at the operating point we are forced to use, the measured gap is 0.85 vs. 0.60: one missed attack roughly every four days versus more than one missed attack per day.

What AUC hides: AUC averages over the whole curve, including high-FPR regions our team can never afford to operate in. Two systems can have similar AUC and very different left edges. The left edge is where we live.

What this evaluation cannot tell us: Because training and test share attack families, this ranking applies to known attacks. A fairer test of novel-attack coverage holds out whole attack families from training, or splits the data by time, and then measures every system on attacks the supervised models never saw. This remains a proxy, since a truly new attack cannot be labeled in advance. Even under such a test, Isolation Forest may still struggle at a budget this tight, because benign traffic that merely looks unusual uses up its false-positive allowance. In practice, unsupervised detection is often given its own alert lane and review budget rather than competing for the same 15 daily slots.

Scenario 2: A High-Consequence OT Network, FPR Budget 0.01

Scenario: Same Systems, Different Organization

You lead security for a power utility's operational technology network. A missed attack on control systems is potentially catastrophic, and the organization staffs 20 analysts across three shifts to match. How does the operating-point decision change?

A bigger budget, deliberately spent: Twenty analysts can absorb roughly 100 investigations per day, so the tolerable FPR rises to about 0.01 (100 / 9,997). At that operating point, System A's measured TPR is 0.98: it misses an attack only rarely, at the cost of roughly 100 false alarms a day that the larger team absorbs by design.

Why this is rational: Here the cost of a false negative (an attack reaching control systems) vastly exceeds the cost of false positives (analyst hours). The organization buys detection coverage with staffing. Same systems, same curves, different operating point, because the operating point is an organizational decision, not a model property.

Common Misconception

"AUC of 0.95 means 95% accurate" is wrong. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen attack example scores higher than a randomly chosen benign example. It measures ranking across all thresholds, not accuracy at any one of them. An AUC of 0.95 is consistent with, for example, a 30% false positive rate at 90% recall, which would bury our 15-alert budget three hundred times over. When a vendor quotes AUC, our question stays the same: what is the false positive rate at the operating point we can sustain?
