1

The Wire-Speed Problem: Where Detection Happens

In Sections 1 and 2, we worked after the fact. Logs already existed, alerts already existed, and our job (with parsers, rules, and an LLM as one assistant among several) was to make sense of them. This section asks the question we skipped: where do those alerts come from in the first place?

The answer is the , and it operates under a constraint none of our earlier tools faced. A busy enterprise link carries millions of packets per second. The decision "is this traffic suspicious?" has to be made in-line, in microseconds, on every packet or connection. No human reads at that speed, and neither does a language model. Whatever fills this slot must be fast, cheap per decision, and predictable.

It helps to see where each tool in this module actually sits. Reading the latency context explains why each stage uses the tools it does!

  1. 1

    Network tap / sensor

    Copies traffic off the wire. Latency budget: microseconds. Tools: hardware, capture drivers.

  2. 2

    Detection (this section)

    Decides what becomes an alert. Latency budget: microseconds to milliseconds. Tools: signature engines, ML classifiers, anomaly detectors.

  3. 3

    Collection and correlation

    Aggregates alerts and logs. Latency budget: seconds. Tools: SIEM, normalization rules (Section 1).

  4. 4

    Triage

    Prioritizes what humans see. Latency budget: seconds to minutes. Tools: correlation rules, SOAR playbooks, LLM assistance (Section 2).

  5. 5

    Analyst investigation

    Confirms or dismisses. Latency budget: minutes to hours. Tools: human judgment, every evidence source available.

Everything we did in Sections 1 and 2 lives in stages 3 through 5. This section is about stage 2, the layer that decides what exists for the rest of the pipeline to analyze. We will not build a detector or train a model here. Building and training these systems is beyond our scope; our goal is to understand them well enough to evaluate them, work with the engineers who run them, and reason about where LLM assistance does and does not fit alongside them.

Signatures First: The Snort and Suricata Baseline

The workhorse of stage 2, in most production networks, is not machine learning of any kind. It is : tools such as and that match traffic against a database of rules written by human analysts. This is the baseline every other approach in this section is measured against, so we start by reading one rule closely.

1alert tcp \?
2 $EXTERNAL_NET any -> $HOME_NET 22 \?
3 (msg:"Possible SSH brute-force attempt"; \?
4 flow:to_server; \?
5 threshold:type both, track by_src, count 5, seconds 60; \?
6 classtype:attempted-recon; sid:1000001; rev:1;)?
Hover or tap a line to see what it does.

Notice what this rule is. It is a precise, human-written statement of one attack pattern, and that gives signature detection three properties worth respecting:

  • Fast: signature engines operate at line rate, processing millions of packets per second. They fit the stage-2 latency budget by design.
  • Deterministic: the same traffic always produces the same result. There is no sampling, no randomness, no version-to-version drift in behavior.
  • Interpretable: when the rule fires, the explanation is the rule. Line 5 tells the analyst exactly why the alert exists.

The limitation is the mirror image of the strength. A signature can only describe what someone has already seen and written down. A genuinely new attack matches no rule and passes through silently. Rule sets also decay: someone must write, test, tune, and retire rules continuously as attacks change. Signature detection is the production baseline not because it is complete, but because its costs and failure modes are well understood. Every approach that follows in this section exists to cover what rules cannot, and each one pays for that coverage somewhere else.

From Packets to Features: Flows and the UNSW-NB15 Schema

To move beyond rules, an ML-based detector needs numeric input. But network traffic does not arrive as numbers in neat columns. It arrives as packets, and the path from packets to model input involves two transformations that we need to understand before any talk of algorithms, because everything a model can and cannot detect is decided here.

Step One: Packets Are Grouped into Flows

Raw traffic can be recorded in full as captures, but full capture is enormous and most of it is irrelevant to any single decision. So monitoring systems summarize. All packets that share the same within a time window are collapsed into one record: one row that describes the whole conversation.

  1. 1

    Packets on the wire

    Headers and payloads, millions per second.

  2. 2

    Flow assembly

    Packets sharing a five-tuple are grouped into one conversation record.

  3. 3

    Feature extraction

    The flow becomes numbers: durations, counts, byte totals, timing statistics. Payload content is discarded here.

  4. 4

    Feature vector

    One row of numbers per flow, e.g. [dur=0.42, spkts=7, sbytes=1834, ...]

  5. 5

    Classifier decision

    The model outputs a label and a score. It has never seen a packet, only the vector.

Here is the transformation made concrete. On the left of the table is what actually crossed the wire for one short connection; on the right is the single flow record it collapses into. We will reuse this exact connection in the worked example in part 5.

What crossed the wire (packet view) What the flow record keeps
10.0.14.55:44312 → 10.0.0.22:22 TCP SYN, then handshake (3 packets), then 4 packets of encrypted SSH negotiation and data, then teardown. Payload bytes: present but encrypted. proto=tcp
dur=0.42 (seconds)
spkts=7, dpkts=6
sbytes=1834, dbytes=1326
sttl=62, dttl=64
sinpkt=58.3 (ms, average)

Read the right column again and notice what is gone: the payload, the credentials tried, the commands run, everything inside the connection. A flow record describes the shape of a conversation, not its content. This loss is permanent for every downstream tool. An encrypted exfiltration and a legitimate large upload can produce nearly identical flow records, and no model consuming flow features can recover the difference. We will see this limit bite in the practice scenarios.

The tradeoff has a compensating benefit. Behavioral shape generalizes across networks better than packet-level detail does. Researchers have found that models trained on attributes of individual packets, such as exact TTL values and precise timings, transferred poorly to other networks, because those attributes are environment-specific. Flow-level aggregates, by contrast, capture patterns such as "many short connections to one service" that look similar wherever they occur.

Step Two: Flows Are Described by a Feature Schema

Which numbers get extracted from each flow? That is defined by a feature , and the parallel to Section 1 is exact. There, log fields had names, types, and definitions set by the log producer, and we learned to read the documentation before trusting a field. Feature sets work the same way: each one is defined and documented by whoever built it, and the documentation is where its meaning lives.

The schema we will work with belongs to UNSW-NB15, a research dataset created at UNSW Sydney (Moustafa and Slay, 2015; details and links on the References page). It defines 49 features per flow, grouped by the authors into categories. This is one research team's documented design, widely used in IDS research; it is not an industry standard, and other tools define different features. The table below shows 15 representative features, using the dataset's own category names, with a column we added: the attack behavior each feature can encode. That last column is the point of this whole exercise.

Feature Description Category (per dataset docs) Attack behavior it can encode
dur Connection duration (seconds) Basic Brute-force attempts are brief; persistent channels are long
spkts Source-to-destination packet count Basic Floods produce extreme packet counts
dpkts Destination-to-source packet count Basic Rejected logins produce small, repetitive replies
sbytes Source-to-destination bytes Basic Uploads out of the network inflate this side
dbytes Destination-to-source bytes Basic Exfiltration shows high sbytes with low dbytes (asymmetry)
sttl Source-to-destination value Basic Hints at sender distance and OS; environment-specific
dttl Destination-to-source TTL value Basic Same, for the responder
sinpkt Source inter-packet arrival time (ms) Time Machine-driven attacks send packets at near-zero intervals
dinpkt Destination inter-packet arrival time (ms) Time Response timing shifts when a server is under load
sjit Source (ms) Time Scripted traffic is steady; human traffic varies
ct_srv_src Connections with same service and source address (last 100 connections) Connection (generated) One source hammering one service: brute-force fingerprint
ct_dst_sport_ltm Connections with same destination address and source port (last 100) Connection (generated) Port reuse patterns typical of scanners and floods
ct_srv_dst Connections with same service and destination address (last 100) Connection (generated) Many connections converging on one service at one host
is_ftp_login Whether the FTP session authenticated (1) or not (0) General purpose (generated) Anonymous vs authenticated FTP activity
ct_flw_http_mthd Count of flows with HTTP methods such as GET and POST General purpose (generated) Visible only for plaintext HTTP; blind inside

Two things deserve a careful look.

First, the "Connection (generated)" features aggregate over a sliding window of the last 100 connections. A single flow cannot reveal that one source opened 87 SSH connections in a minute; a windowed count can. This is how flow records come to encode behavior over time.

Second, every row of that last column is analyst knowledge frozen into arithmetic. Someone knew brute-force looks like one source hammering one service, and built ct_srv_src so a model could see it. This is , and it is where detection quality is mostly decided. The model never sees the network; it sees what the features let it see.

The Ceiling Principle

Features set the ceiling on what any model can detect. The algorithm only determines how close to that ceiling you get. Two strong algorithms given the same good features perform similarly; the best algorithm in the world given features that miss the signal detects nothing.

Three Detectors Compared: Rules, Supervised ML, Anomaly Detection

With the data layer clear, we can compare the detection approaches honestly. We already know the rule-based baseline from part 2. The two ML approaches differ in one fundamental way: what they learn from.

learns from labeled flows: each training example carries a verdict (attack or benign) established in advance. Tree-ensemble algorithms such as and are common choices because flow features are tabular data. Published studies on UNSW-NB15 report strong results, with scores varying by train/test split and metric (see the References page). Three costs come with those scores: labels are expensive to produce, labels go stale as attacks change, and a supervised model only recognizes attack types it was trained on. A genuinely new attack is invisible to it, exactly as it is to a rule.

learns from unlabeled traffic. It models what normal looks like for a given network, then flags deviations. This is the only approach with a real chance against novel attacks, including , because it never needed to see the attack in advance. The price is the false alarm budget: anomalous does not mean malicious. New applications, holiday traffic, and software updates all deviate from the baseline and get flagged, and every such flag consumes analyst time (a , in Section 2's vocabulary).

Property Rule-based / signatures Supervised ML Unsupervised anomaly
Learns from Human-written rules Labeled attack and benign flows Unlabeled traffic (its own baseline)
Known attacks Strong, if a rule exists Strong on trained categories Weaker; sees them only as deviations
Novel attacks Blind Blind Possible, if the attack looks unusual
False positive profile Low, tunable per rule Moderate, depends on training quality Highest; "unusual" happens constantly
Interpretability The rule is the explanation Partial (feature importances) Low ("this deviated" without a category)
Ongoing cost Rule writing and tuning Labeling and retraining Baseline upkeep, alert review capacity

Research comparing these approaches consistently finds the pattern the grid suggests: supervised methods win on known attack types, unsupervised methods provide the only coverage for truly novel ones, and rules remain the cheap, predictable backbone. No single row of strengths covers the threat landscape, which is why real deployments layer them. Where an LLM fits into this picture is a question we take up later in this section and in the next, after we have seen these detectors work, and fail, on concrete traffic!
