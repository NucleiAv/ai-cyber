1

Ten Thousand Events, Three Analysts

Before we compare any tools, we need to be honest about the problem they are supposed to solve. Throughout this section we will work with one running set of numbers, typical of a small :

  • About 10,000 security-relevant events per day reach our triage queue after collection and .
  • About 3 of those events per day, on average, are genuinely malicious.
  • Our 3 analysts can investigate about 15 alerts per day between them before investigation quality degrades.

Those numbers define a hard constraint. Whatever detection stack we run, it must compress 10,000 events into roughly 15 investigations without losing the 3 that matter. Every alert past the budget is skimmed, deferred, or ignored, and that is how turns a problem into a problem.

"Which tool is better?" is not a useful question. The useful question, which we will answer step by step, is: which combination of tools gets the 3 real attacks into the 15 investigations, at a cost we can sustain?

2

The Baseline We Already Run

No serious SOC starts from zero. Before any machine learning or language model enters the picture, three conventional layers are already on duty, and any new tool must beat or complement them, not replace them by assumption.

Signature detection. In Section 3 we read an annotated Snort rule line by line. A is a precise, human-written description of one known attack pattern. Signature engines such as and Suricata are fast (line rate), deterministic (the same traffic always produces the same result), and interpretable (the explanation for the alert is the rule itself). On known patterns their false positive rate can be tuned very low, which is exactly what our 15-alert budget needs.

Correlation rules. Our runs that combine events across sources: five failed logins followed by a success, a new account creation followed by a privilege change. Like signatures, they encode human knowledge and are auditable after the fact.

Human review. Analysts are the existing decision layer. They carry the organizational context no tool has: which server is due for maintenance, which vendor scans us on Tuesdays, which alert pattern preceded last year's incident.

The baseline's limits are the mirror image of its strengths. Signatures and correlation rules only describe what someone has already seen and written down, and rule sets decay unless they are continuously maintained. The table below previews the traffic we will follow for the rest of this section, and shows where the baseline needs help.

Traffic What the baseline does Why
SSH brute-force attempts Caught The Section 3 Snort rule describes the pattern exactly: 5 connections in 60 seconds.
Known-malware downloads Caught Hash and URL signatures exist and are updated from feeds.
Small periodic transfers to a domain registered last week Slips through No signature describes this domain. No correlation rule was written for this interval pattern. Volumes are too small to trip thresholds.

The Alert Record: Our Data Contract

Before we can chain tools together, we have to agree on what they hand each other. Three different data shapes move through our pipeline, and confusing them is a common source of design errors:

  • Numeric flow features. Engineered values such as packet counts, byte totals, and interval statistics, derived from the . This is what classical ML models consume.
  • The structured alert record. A machine-readable record that every detector writes and every downstream stage reads. Ours is serialized as .
  • Free-text rationale. Human-readable explanation attached to an alert for the analyst. Never parsed by code, never used as a decision input by machines.

JSON Is Syntax, Not a Security Standard

A tidy JSON alert does not supply its own meaning: JSON defines punctuation, not semantics. Severity is the test case. uses a 0–7 scale (0 most urgent), a 0–10 scale, and our alert record a four-word house scale. Three formats, three incompatible vocabularies; mapping between them is a normalization decision we document in our . Section 1 covers syslog and CEF in detail.

The alert record is our pipeline's contract: every stage we add in parts 4 and 5 reads this record, writes back only fields it owns, and passes through before the next stage trusts it. With the contract pinned down, we can start adding detection layers.

What Classical ML Adds to the Baseline

Section 3 built the detection mechanics; here we place them in the pipeline. A consumes the numeric flow features from part 3 and adds the one thing rules cannot offer: detection of patterns nobody has written down.

generalize from labeled examples of known attack families, so they can catch variants that evade an exact signature. learn a baseline of normal traffic and flag statistical outliers, which gives us a chance against patterns absent from any training set, at the price of more false positives.

The costs are real and recurring. Supervised models need labeled data, which is expensive to produce. Both kinds degrade as traffic and attacker behavior change, the problem, so retraining is a scheduled maintenance task, not an emergency fix. And neither kind can explain itself in operational terms or apply organizational context. An anomaly score of 0.92 is not an explanation.

This version of the pipeline already fits our volume constraint. What it leaves unsolved is the analyst's first question about every alert in the queue: why is this suspicious?

5

One More Tool, Not a Replacement: LLM-Assisted Triage

Only now, with the baseline and ML layer in place, do we add , for one specific job: second-pass work on the already-reduced alert queue. Given an alert record and the raw log lines it references, an can draft a summary, suggest a severity, and connect related alerts into a narrative. That is the entire job we give it, and it comes with four limits we state up front:

  • Latency. Seconds to minutes per alert. An LLM can never sit at line rate, so it can never be the primary detection layer. It works here only because parts 2 and 4 already shrank the volume.
  • Generated rationales are not evidence. The explanation an LLM writes is fluent text produced by the same process that produces its mistakes, including . Section 2 catalogued the failure modes. A rationale is a draft to verify, not a finding.
  • No quantitative threshold. We tune an ML detector by moving a numeric threshold. An LLM's behavior is steered by prompt changes, which are coarser and harder to measure.
  • A knowledge cutoff. The model knows nothing newer than its training data. Yesterday's attack infrastructure is invisible to it unless we put the evidence in the prompt.

With three tool families on the table, we can compare them honestly, dimension by dimension.

Dimension Signatures & correlation rules Classical ML LLM-based analysis
Input Packets, events, log fields Engineered numeric features Text: alert records, log excerpts
Speed Line rate Milliseconds Seconds to minutes per alert
Tunability Edit, add, retire rules Numeric threshold on a continuum Prompt changes; coarse, hard to measure
Known patterns Excellent; very low false positives Good; generalizes to variants Unnecessary; earlier layers already catch these
Novel patterns Blind by design Unsupervised: flags outliers, with more false positives May surface contextual leads; unreliable, treat as hypotheses
Explanation The rule is the explanation Scores and feature weights; limited Readable rationale; generated text that requires validation
Maintenance Continuous rule curation Labeled data, scheduled retraining Prompt upkeep, model upgrades, cutoff limits
Place in our pipeline First pass, full volume First pass, full volume Second pass, flagged subset only

One stage of the diagram deserves emphasis. The analyst's verification is what makes the LLM's drafts usable at all. This is the same cycle from Section 3, now applied to generated text: the model drafts, an independent check settles disputed claims, and the verdict becomes a labeled example that improves the next round.
