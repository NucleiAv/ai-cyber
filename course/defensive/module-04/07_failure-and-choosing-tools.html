When the Pipeline Fails: The Radiology Workstation Incident

The pipeline we built places AI in the detection and enrichment stages, with people making the decisions. What happens when that boundary erodes in practice? The following composite scenario is fictional, but every element of it is drawn from documented incident patterns.

A regional hospital network deploys an LLM triage assistant in its SOC. It reduces triage time noticeably in the first three months. Analysts come to rely on its severity suggestions, especially overnight, when one analyst covers the whole queue.

1

02:14 · Detection works (no failure)

The ML layer raises a critical alert: anomalous traffic from a radiology workstation to the . The detection stack did its job.

2

02:14 · Enrichment fails (stage 2 failure)

The LLM assistant classifies the alert: "Low: routine network scan, likely vulnerability assessment tool." The rationale is fluent and wrong, the part-6 step-5 failure arriving at the worst hour.

3

02:15 · Verification fails (stage 3 failure)

The overnight analyst reads "Low," accepts it without checking the evidence, and moves on. The evidence-citation check from part 6, which would have shown the rationale citing nothing, never happens.

4

02:15–14:00 · Dwell time (organizational failure)

For twelve hours the attacker moves unimpeded. The SMB traffic was toward the domain controller. No escalation policy required a second look at a critical-detection/low-triage mismatch, and overnight staffing guaranteed nobody had time for one.

5

14:00 · Impact

encrypts patient records, imaging systems, and billing. Surgical schedules are disrupted. Regulators ask the how a critical alert at 02:14 became an enterprise outage at 14:00.

After You Have Written Your Response

Write your brief first. Then compare it against what a 'trained' response tends to address.

A trained response avoids assigning blame to a single entity and analyzes the failure chain: the LLM misclassification was a stage-2 failure, the analyst's uncritical acceptance was a stage-3 failure, and staffing plus the absence of an escalation rule for detection/triage severity mismatches created the conditions for both. It proposes concrete gates, for example: any alert where the detector says critical and the triage layer says low gets mandatory human evidence review.

An untrained (they didn't take the right classes!) response blames the LLM alone (ignoring that the design assumed analyst verification) or the analyst alone (ignoring that the system was sold as load reduction, and that trust calibration degrades predictably under fatigue and understaffing).

Choosing Tools on Evidence

Everything in this section compresses to a discipline of four questions. We will reuse them every time a new detection tool, AI or otherwise, asks for a place in our pipeline:

  • 1. What does the baseline already catch? If signatures and correlation rules cover the pattern, the new tool is solving a solved problem.
  • 2. What does the candidate add at the operating point we can afford? Measured on our data, at our FPR budget. Not AUC, not a vendor benchmark, not a demo.
  • 3. How is its output validated against ground truth? Name the gates: schema checks, independent lookups, evidence-citation review. A tool whose claims cannot be checked cannot be trusted with decisions.
  • 4. Who is accountable for the decision it informs? A person, named in the process, with the time and authority to verify. If the answer is "nobody, it is automatic," stop.

Where This Goes Next

The parse-classify-validate pattern we built in this module is not specific to intrusion detection. In Module 5 we will apply the same structure to threat intelligence: parsing unstructured reports, classifying indicators of compromise, and validating extracted intelligence, with the six-step trace from part 6 as our template.

The feature and labeling discipline from Section 3 also carries forward. In Module 7, when we fine-tune a model with for the midterm project, training-data quality will depend on the same judgment that separates informative features from noise in an IDS dataset. Knowing what makes a feature informative, and what introduces bias or leakage, is exactly what we will need to curate and label fine-tuning data well.

Our pipeline uses a single LLM at the enrichment stage. Researchers are exploring multi-agent architectures in which several specialized LLM agents divide triage, investigation, and reporting among themselves and exchange findings as structured messages. One example is the CORTEX proposal; note that it is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed (reference page, E7). Vendors describe related visions of largely autonomous SOC agents; those are roadmaps and marketing documents, not evaluated systems (reference page, E8). Industry surveys report broad interest in these approaches (reference page, E9).

Our four questions do not change. If anything, question 4 gets harder: when several agents hand claims to each other before a person sees anything, accountability and validation get more complex, not less. This is an area to watch with the same discipline we apply to every tool, not an area to deploy uncritically.
