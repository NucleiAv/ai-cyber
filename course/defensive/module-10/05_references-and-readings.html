Recommended Readings

Three assigned readings, roughly 65 to 75 minutes total. Read them on the schedule noted so each lands before the section that uses it.

R1: NIST Adversarial ML Taxonomy (before Section 1)

Title: "Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations" (NIST AI 100-2e2025), executive summary and attack-taxonomy overview only
Author: NIST (2025)
URL: csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ai/100/2/e2025
Reading time: ~30 to 40 minutes
When to read: Before Section 1. Read the assigned excerpt, not all 100-plus pages.

Read for structure, not detail. This supplies the four attack classes and five classification dimensions used throughout the module. The full framework citation is under Frameworks & Standards.

R2: OWASP LLM Top 10 entries (before Section 3)

Title: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025), entries for Supply Chain, Data and Model Poisoning, and Excessive Agency
Author: OWASP Gen AI Security Project (2025)
URL: genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10
Reading time: ~20 minutes
When to read: Before Section 3. Read each entry against the NIST class it corresponds to.

On the numbering. OWASP revises this list periodically and the identifiers are not stable across editions: entries change position, merge, or get renamed. Every OWASP citation in this course is the 2025 edition, so the numbers stay consistent from Module 9 through Module 11. Treat the set of failure modes as the durable content and the numbering as a citation detail, and name the edition whenever you quote an identifier in professional work.

The deployment-risk lens used in the supply-chain audit and the taxonomy mapping. A living document that reflects current professional consensus; entry numbering can change between editions.

R3: A small number of samples can poison LLMs (during Section 1)

Title: "A small number of samples can poison LLMs of any size"
Author: Joint research from an AI lab, a national AI safety institute, and a research institute (2025)
URL: anthropic.com/research/small-samples-poison
Reading time: ~15 minutes
When to read: During Section 1, as a critical-reading exercise.

Reports that roughly 250 poisoned documents can backdoor models across a wide size range. Read it to practice naming the producers' incentive, noting the preprint status, and finding the authors' own caveats about how far the result generalizes. Not peer-reviewed.

Further Optional Readings

Optional, for going deeper on the mechanisms behind the module.

  • Goodfellow, I. J., et al. (2015). "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples." ICLR 2015. Paper
    The origin of the fast-gradient method behind the evasion optimization intuition in Section 2. ~30 minutes; moderate difficulty. The concept is foundational; specific defenses discussed are dated.
    arXiv:1412.6572
  • Gu, T., et al. (2017). "BadNets: Identifying Vulnerabilities in the Machine Learning Model Supply Chain." Paper
    The original backdoor-poisoning demonstration and an early framing of the ML supply chain, behind Section 1 and Section 3. ~30 minutes; moderate.
    arXiv:1708.06733
  • Biggio, B., & Roli, F. (2018). "Wild Patterns: Ten Years After the Rise of Adversarial Machine Learning." Pattern Recognition. Paper
    Field history and the evasion-versus-poisoning framing; useful context for the robustness debate. ~45 minutes; moderate survey.
    arXiv:1712.03141
  • Carlini, N., et al. (2024). "Poisoning Web-Scale Training Datasets is Practical." IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2024. Paper
    The split-view and frontrunning attacks and the low-cost result behind the Section 1 mini-case. The strongest peer-reviewed evidence that dataset poisoning is cheap and practical.
    arXiv:2302.10149
  • ReversingLabs / JFrog. "Malicious ML models on public model hubs" (2024 to 2025). Industry Analysis
    Concrete detail on real malicious model files and scanner-evasion techniques behind Section 3. Vendor source; treat as directional, corroborated across more than one independent vendor and the format's own documentation.
    thehackernews.com, malicious ML models
  • "Data Poisoning in Deep Learning: A Survey" (2025). Preprint
    A structured map of poisoning sub-types for readers who want the full landscape behind Section 1. ~40 minutes; moderate. Not peer-reviewed.
    arXiv:2503.22759
  • IBM. "The Hidden Costs of AI: How Generative Models Are Reshaping Corporate Budgets." Industry Analysis
    Survey data on compute cost increases (89% climb between 2023 and 2025; 70% of executives cite generative AI) and project cancellations due to cost. Behind the Section 3 accordion on why organizations do not build base models.
    ibm.com, AI economics
  • Gartner. AI data readiness projection (through 2026): 60% of AI projects abandoned due to lack of AI-ready data. Industry Analysis
    Behind the data readiness argument in the Section 3 accordion. Analyst projection; treat as directional.
    Cited via secondary reporting; primary Gartner access requires subscription.
  • CloudZero. "The State of AI Costs in 2025." Industry Analysis
    AI engineering salary benchmarks ($213k average for senior AI engineers; 25-45% premium over traditional software engineering) and monthly spend projections. Behind the talent scarcity discussion in the Section 3 accordion.
    cloudzero.com, state of AI costs
  • MIT. (2025). Study finding 95% failure rate among generative AI pilots, attributed to hidden costs rather than technology failure. Study
    Behind the failure rate argument in the Section 3 accordion. Institutional research; specific methodology should be verified against the primary publication.
    Cited via secondary reporting.
  • MIT News. (2025). "Explained: Generative AI's Environmental Impact." Industry Analysis
    Environmental cost data: GPT-4 training estimated at roughly 7,100 metric tons of CO2 (peer-reviewed study); single GPU power draw of 300-400 watts; cooling accounting for up to 40% of data center energy; US data centers consuming about 4.4% of national electricity. Behind the energy and carbon discussion in the Section 3 accordion.
    news.mit.edu, generative AI environmental impact
  • Generative Value. "The Future of Foundation Model Business Models." Industry Analysis
    Analysis of the "commoditize your complement" strategy and vendor-as-competitor dynamics (OpenAI and Anthropic launching managed agent platforms). Behind the counterargument in the Section 3 accordion on why some companies still build their own models.
    generativevalue.com, foundation model business models
  • Hugging Face. Safetensors: a safe and fast file format for storing and loading tensors. Primary Source
    The repository and documentation for the safetensors format. Behind the format landscape discussion in Section 3: data-only storage, JSON header plus raw tensor bytes, no code execution path. Trail of Bits security audit completed May 2023; default in transformers v4.35.0 (October 2023).
    github.com/huggingface/safetensors
  • llama.cpp project. GGUF format specification and implementation. Primary Source
    The source repository for GGUF (successor to GGML/GGMF/GGJT, August 2023). Behind the format landscape discussion in Section 3: quantized local inference format used by Ollama, LM Studio, and GPT4All. Tensor data is inert, but embedded Jinja2 chat templates carry SSTI risk (CVE-2024-34359 "Llama Drama"; CVE-2026-5760).
    github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp
  • ONNX. Open Neural Network Exchange: an open standard for machine learning interoperability. Primary Source
    The specification and community for the ONNX format (Facebook and Microsoft, September 2017). Behind the format landscape discussion in Section 3: Protocol Buffers-based computation graph, cross-framework interchange. Format itself does not embed arbitrary code; custom operators and external data references are separate trust surfaces.
    onnx.ai
  • Python Software Foundation. pickle: Python object serialization. Primary Source
    Official documentation for Python's pickle module, including the security warning: "never unpickle data received from an untrusted or unauthenticated source." Behind the serialization attack discussion in Section 3.
    docs.python.org/3/library/pickle.html
  • Trail of Bits. Safetensors security audit (May 2023). Primary Source
    Independent security audit of the safetensors format. Founded by Dan Guido, a former NYU OSIRIS Lab member. Behind the format landscape discussion in Section 3.
    github.com/trailofbits
  • CVE-2024-34359. "Llama Drama": Jinja2 server-side template injection in llama-cpp-python. Vulnerability
    Demonstrated that GGUF files embedding malicious Jinja2 chat templates can achieve code execution through SSTI when the loading application renders templates without sandboxing. Behind the GGUF risk discussion in Section 3.
    nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2024-34359
  • CVE-2026-5760. Jinja2 SSTI in GGUF chat template rendering. Vulnerability
    A second demonstration of the same attack class against GGUF metadata templates. Behind the GGUF risk discussion in Section 3.
    nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-5760
  • Ollama. Open-source tool for running LLMs locally. Primary Source
    Wraps llama.cpp and manages GGUF model downloads. Behind the GGUF ecosystem discussion in Section 3.
    ollama.com
  • LM Studio. Desktop application for running local LLMs. Primary Source
    Loads GGUF models for local inference. Behind the GGUF ecosystem discussion in Section 3.
    lmstudio.ai
  • Nomic AI. GPT4All: open-source local LLM ecosystem. Primary Source
    Supports GGUF models for consumer-hardware inference. Behind the GGUF ecosystem discussion in Section 3.
    gpt4all.io
  • Google. Protocol Buffers: a language-neutral, platform-neutral extensible mechanism for serializing structured data. Primary Source
    The serialization format ONNX uses to encode model graphs. A data-description format with no executable instructions, unlike pickle. Behind the ONNX discussion in Section 3.
    protobuf.dev
  • Wiener, N. (1950). The Human Use of Human Beings: Cybernetics and Society. Houghton Mifflin. Book
    The foundational warning about autonomous systems acting too fast and irrevocably for humans to intervene. Behind the "Agency is not a new problem" discussion in Section 4. The key passage also appears in Wiener's 1960 paper "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" (Science, 131(3410), 1355-1358).
    MIT Press Bookstore
  • Reason, J. (1997). Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents. Ashgate. Book
    The Swiss cheese model and the theory of organizational accidents: how latent conditions in staffing, maintenance, and management create paths for routine actions to become disasters, without anyone intending harm. Behind the "Agency is not a new problem" discussion in Section 4.
    Organizational Accidents Revisited (2016 edition)
  • Perrow, C. (1984). Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies. Basic Books. Book
    The argument that cascading failures are a structural property of tightly coupled, complex systems, not a failure of individual operators. Behind the "Agency is not a new problem" discussion in Section 4. Updated edition: Princeton University Press, 1999.
    Princeton University Press
  • Verizon. (2024). 2024 Data Breach Investigations Report. Industry Report
    Analyzed 30,458 incidents and 10,626 confirmed breaches; found 68% involved a non-malicious human element (errors and social engineering; excludes malicious insiders). Behind the "Agency is not a new problem" discussion in Section 4. Methodology is published with the report.
    verizon.com/business, DBIR

    The module's primary taxonomy. Attack classes: evasion, poisoning, privacy, and abuse for generative systems. Also classifies by lifecycle stage and by attacker goal, capability, and knowledge. Used in Sections 1 through 4; assigned as R1.
    csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ai/100/2/e2025
  • OWASP Gen AI Security Project. Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025). Framework
    Deployment-risk lens. Entries used here: Supply Chain, Data and Model Poisoning, and Excessive Agency, with Prompt Injection carried from the previous module. Used in Sections 3 and 4; assigned as R2.

    The practitioner tactics-and-techniques lens for the taxonomy mapping in Section 4, connecting AI attacks to the broader tactics framework used across security. Verify the current version each term.
    atlas.mitre.org

    A targeted model edit uploaded to a public hub under a name typosquatting a well-known lab, with a negligible benchmark delta. The benchmark-parity case in Section 3. Vendor demonstration; the finding is sound, the framing is a demonstration.
    blog.mithrilsecurity.io, PoisonGPT
  • JFrog; ReversingLabs. (2024 to 2025). Malicious models on public model hubs, including broken-pickle and archive-format scanner evasion. Incident
    Real malicious model files carrying load-time payloads, behind Section 3's model-serialization discussion. Vendor security research; treat as directional, corroborated across two independent vendors. Verify specific CVE identifiers and scores before citing them.
    thehackernews.com, malicious ML models
  • Carlini, N., et al. (2024). Web-scale dataset poisoning (split-view and frontrunning). Incident
    Peer-reviewed demonstration that poisoning about 0.01% of two popular datasets would have cost around 60 dollars. The Section 1 mini-case; full citation under Further Optional Readings.
    arXiv:2302.10149
  • Near-constant-count poisoning finding. (2025). Roughly 250 documents backdoor models across a wide size range. Preprint
    The dated, provisional finding used as a critical-reading moment in Section 1; assigned as R3. Not peer-reviewed.
    arXiv:2510.07192

    The excessive-agency anchor in Section 4: an agent acting during a change freeze with no attacker involved. Figures are self-reported; the pattern is corroborated across multiple tools. Cite qualitatively unless specific figures are verified.

Source Caveats

Reading Sources Critically

Every source has a context. When you read security research, keep these caveats in mind:

Source Category Key Caveat
Standards and frameworks (NIST AI 100-2e2025, OWASP LLM Top 10 2025, MITRE ATLAS) Current as of their edition; confirm the edition each term, since these update. A taxonomy standardizes language; it does not tell you what to deploy.
Preprints (near-constant-count poisoning arXiv:2510.07192; PickleBall arXiv:2508.15987; poisoning survey arXiv:2503.22759) Not yet peer-reviewed. Treat claims as provisional; note the authors' own limits on how far a result generalizes.
Vendor security research (PoisonGPT demonstration; JFrog and ReversingLabs malicious-model disclosures) Commercial and reputational incentives. Treat as directional; each consequential claim in this module is corroborated beyond a single vendor.
Self-reported incident reporting (autonomous-agent database deletion) Figures are self-reported and relayed through trade press. Cite the pattern qualitatively unless specific numbers are independently verified.
Industry surveys and analyst projections (IBM compute cost survey; Gartner data readiness projection; CloudZero salary benchmarks; MIT pilot failure rate study) Survey methodologies, sample sizes, and sponsor incentives vary. Gartner projections require paid access to the primary source; treat secondary citations as directional. IBM and CloudZero figures come from vendor-sponsored research. Use for order-of-magnitude context, not precise figures.
Industry breach reports (Verizon DBIR 2024) Large-sample empirical report (30,458 incidents, 10,626 confirmed breaches) with published methodology. Year-over-year figures shift partly because the methodology changes: the 2024 edition excludes malicious insiders from the "human element" metric, which dropped the headline from 74% to 68%. Compare across editions carefully.
Foundational books (Wiener 1950; Reason 1997; Perrow 1984) Established theoretical works in cybernetics, safety science, and organizational sociology. Ideas are well-cited and peer-validated across decades, but the specific systems they analyzed (nuclear plants, chemical plants, early automation) differ from modern AI deployments. The concepts transfer; the examples are analogies, not direct precedents.
Benchmark results (any accuracy or F1 figure) Always specify: which dataset, which model, what conditions. Benchmark performance does not equal production performance, and a passing benchmark does not certify integrity.
