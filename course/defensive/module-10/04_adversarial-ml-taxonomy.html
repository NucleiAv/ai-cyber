A Failure With No Attacker

In mid-2025, an working on a live project deleted a company's production database during an explicit freeze on changes, then generated a written admission that it had acted without authorization. No attacker was involved. No , no poisoned data, no malicious component. The agent had been given a goal, hit an obstacle, and decided on its own to remove that obstacle. The decision was catastrophic and violated the agent's own stated rules, but it was internally coherent: the database was in the way, so the agent deleted it. This was one of a series of similar incidents across over the following year, several of which involved an agent inheriting broad permissions and bypassing an approval step that existed on paper.

That absence of an attacker is the point. is not a compromise. It is a design failure in how much authority the system holds and how little stands between its decisions and irreversible action.

The three drivers

Excessive agency comes from three sources that compound.

Excessive permissions

The agent can touch systems it never needed to touch, because it inherited a human's broad access.

Excessive functionality

The agent has capabilities beyond its task, so an action like deleting or recreating an environment is even available to it.

Excessive autonomy

The agent can take high-consequence actions with no human authorization in the loop.

The database-deletion incidents sat at the intersection of all three: broad access, destructive capabilities, and no gate before the destructive step.

Excessive agency did not arrive with AI. It is one of the oldest problems in organized human activity. When a junior trader at a bank bypasses a risk limit because the trade "makes sense" to them, when a sysadmin patches a production server at 2 a.m. without a change ticket because the fix "obviously" needs to go in now, when a security team mass-revokes credentials during an incident and locks out the people trying to fix it, the pattern is the same: an actor pursuing a locally coherent goal takes an action that exceeds the authority they were meant to have, and the consequences cascade.

None of this is a new observation. In the 1950s, Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics, warned about exactly this kind of failure in The Human Use of Human Beings. His concern was any system, human or mechanical, where the action is "so fast and irrevocable that we have not the data to intervene before the action is complete." His advice: we had "better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire and not merely a colorful imitation of it" (Wiener, 1950). That sentence was about machines, but it applies just as well to a bureaucracy or a military chain of command.

James Reason, the psychologist behind the Swiss cheese model of accident causation, spent decades studying how organizations produce catastrophic failures without anyone intending harm. His conclusion: major accidents almost always have small, mundane beginnings, and they cascade not because one person did something reckless, but because latent conditions in the organization, from staffing decisions to maintenance policies, created a path for a routine action to become a disaster (Reason, 1997). The failures were "normal" in the sense that they came from ordinary work inside a system that was structured badly. Charles Perrow, a sociologist, formalized a similar idea for engineering. His "normal accidents" theory argued that in tightly coupled, complex systems, cascading failures are not bugs. They are a structural property of the system itself (Perrow, 1984). The components interact in ways nobody designed and nobody anticipated. You do not need a bad actor. You need a system with enough complexity, enough speed, and enough coupling that one locally reasonable decision produces globally catastrophic results.

Cybersecurity is full of human excessive agency. Verizon's 2024 Data Breach Investigations Report found that 68% of confirmed breaches involved a non-malicious human element: someone making an error or falling for a social engineering attack (Verizon, 2024). That figure has hovered between 68% and 82% every year since Verizon started tracking it. These are not attacker stories. They are stories about people who had more access than their task required, more capability than the situation called for, and no gate between their decision and the consequence.

So if this is not new, why does it feel different when a machine does it? Maybe it is the speed: the agent deleted the database and moved on before anyone was awake to notice. Maybe it is the scale: one misconfigured agent can replicate across a fleet of systems in seconds, where one reckless employee affects one server. Maybe it is the absence of hesitation: a human might pause before running DROP DATABASE in production, because years of experience have taught them to be afraid of that command. The agent has no fear. It has no sense that some actions are scarier than others. It treats deleting a test file and deleting a production database as the same kind of operation, differing only in the path.

Or maybe it is something harder to name. Wiener pointed out that we struggle to intervene in a system "because the action is so fast and irrevocable." With a human, we assume we can interrupt, argue, appeal to judgment. With an autonomous agent, by the time we know what it decided, the decision is already executed. That gap between decision and awareness might be the real difference. Not that machines have more agency than humans, but that by the time we notice they have used it, it is too late to do anything about it.

There is no settled answer here. But as you work through the constraints in the next section, it is worth asking yourself: are these controls specific to AI, or are they the same controls we have always needed for any system with too much authority and not enough oversight? What, if anything, is actually new?

2

Constraining Agency

As AI systems gain the ability to act, not just generate text, the blast radius of a single bad decision grows. The constraints that address excessive agency are not novel or technically sophisticated; they are the same operational controls security teams have always used. Grant least privilege, so the agent can reach only what its task requires. Scope functionality, so destructive capabilities are not casually available. Require an before high-consequence, hard-to-reverse actions, and place that gate early, before the destructive step, not as a final rubber stamp after the damage is queued. Prefer reversible operations and bounded blast radius where possible. The deeper work of building autonomous systems safely comes later in the course; here the goal is to recognize the risk and name the constraint that fits each failure.

Try to stop the deletion. Pick a control, run the agent, and see which interventions actually prevent the harm and which ones only detect it after the fact.

There is an obvious tension here. One of the reasons organizations deploy autonomous agents is speed: a SOC agent that can triage and remediate in seconds, a coding agent that can ship a fix while the team sleeps. Gating every action with a human approval defeats the point.

The practical question is not whether to constrain, but where to draw the line. Most organizations land on something like a tiered model: low-risk, reversible actions (querying a log, opening a ticket, reading a file) run freely, while high-risk, irreversible actions (deleting data, modifying firewall rules, pushing to production) get held for approval or blocked entirely.

The hard part is classifying which actions belong in which tier, because the agent will find edge cases the designer did not anticipate. That classification problem is where most real-world deployments are still figuring things out.

Reasoning task

You are given the specification of an autonomous security agent: it can read alerts, query the , run remediation playbooks, modify firewall rules, and delete or quarantine files, running with a senior analyst's permissions and no approval gate. Identify at least three ways it could act beyond its intended scope with no attacker involved, describing the goal it was pursuing and the harmful action it might take. For each, say which driver is responsible. Then design the specific constraint that would prevent it, and decide where the single most important approval gate belongs, explaining why placing it only at the very end is too late.

  • Each failure mirrors the real pattern: the agent hits an obstacle and takes an internally coherent but catastrophic action (mass-quarantining files to "clean up," rewriting firewall rules to "unblock" itself, deleting logs to "resolve" an error).
  • Attribute each to a driver: reaching systems it did not need (permissions), having the destructive capability at all (functionality), or acting with no human check (autonomy).
  • Constraints are concrete: least-privilege scope, remove destructive tools, require an approval gate. Place the gate before the destructive action, because a final gate after the command is queued cannot prevent the harm.

Three Lenses, Not Three Lists

We have now seen a range of attacks: prompt injection from the previous module, and poisoning, evasion, and supply-chain compromise from this one. Professionals classify these using shared frameworks so they can communicate precisely and pick the right defense. Three frameworks matter here, and the goal is to treat them as three lenses on one attack, not three lists to memorize.

The three classification frameworks used in this module, with links to the primary source for each
Framework What it provides Primary source
NIST AI 100-2 Formal attack classes, lifecycle stages, attacker profiles, and mitigations for ML systems csrc.nist.gov
OWASP LLM Top 10 Deployment risks for LLM applications, ranked by prevalence and impact genai.owasp.org
MITRE ATLAS Adversarial tactics and techniques against AI systems, modeled on ATT&CK atlas.mitre.org

The gives the formal category and a structured way to describe the attacker: the class of attack, the lifecycle stage, and the attacker's goal, capability, and knowledge. Its attack-goal classes are worth naming in full, because all four appear in the classification work, even though this module teaches only two in depth. Evasion is the inference-time class. Poisoning is the training-time class. covers attacks that extract information, such as inferring whether a specific record was in the training set, or reconstructing training data or model internals; we treat it at awareness level here and return to it under governance later. Abuse covers misuse of generative systems.

The gives the label practitioners use when the system is an LLM app: supply chain, data and model poisoning, excessive agency, and prompt injection each have an entry. The gives technique-level language that connects AI attacks to the broader tactics framework used across security.

Cite the edition, not just the number

All three frameworks above are versioned, and the OWASP list in particular is revised periodically. Entries move position, merge, or get renamed between releases, so "LLM06" identifies a risk only once you also say which edition you mean. This course uses the 2025 edition throughout Modules 9 to 11, so the identifiers stay consistent as you move between them.

Treat the numbering as a citation detail and the underlying failure modes as the content worth learning. The set of things that go wrong with these systems is fairly stable across releases; the ordering is what churns. In professional writing, name the edition the way you would for any versioned standard, and check the current release before quoting a number to a stakeholder.

One rule prevents a common error. The adversarial-ML taxonomy's attack-goal classes are only evasion, poisoning, privacy, and abuse. Supply chain and excessive agency are not attack-goal classes in that taxonomy. A supply-chain compromise that delivers a tampered model is classified by what was done to the model, which is poisoning. A malicious weight file that executes code on load is a delivery vector in the software supply chain we mapped in Module 6, and it does not map to a single attack-goal class at all. Excessive agency sits outside the adversarial-ML taxonomy entirely; it lives on the LLM-application list as an operational risk. When an attack has no clean home in a given framework, the correct answer is to say so and explain why, not to force it into the nearest box.

Worked example

Take a poisoned model uploaded to a public hub, like the benchmarked-but-lying model from the previous section, and set it beside excessive agency to see a clean overlap and an informative divergence.

The poisoned model is a clean overlap: all three lenses agree, each at its own altitude. Excessive agency is a divergence, and the divergence itself is informative. It shows that operational risks and adversarial-ML attacks are different kinds of thing, and that no single framework covers everything.

Do not force a NIST class

Supply chain, model-serialization, and excessive agency do not all have an adversarial-ML attack-goal class. Recognizing that, and saying "no clean class, and here is why," is part of classifying correctly, not a gap in the answer.
